When a producer first asked Ryūsuke Hamaguchi to adapt a Murakami story, the director thought it over and decided it was too hard. Then he remembered a different one, where only real things happen. The film that came out of it, Drive My Car, won the Oscar for Best International Feature in 2022, with some depth borrowed back from two other Murakami stories. What is left for a writer this famous to risk?

This volume covers 2013 to 2025: a plain, realist novel after the wildness of 1Q84; one passage about the Nanjing Massacre that drew public attacks at home; a story he had called a failure in 1980, rewritten at seventy-four as a third attempt; a Nobel Prize guessing game built on bookmakers' odds; and a film that carried his name further than his own prose had. You do not need the earlier volumes to follow it.

This book is for you if:

  • You loved Drive My Car and want to know how much of it is Murakami
  • You have read Colorless Tsukuru Tazaki, Killing Commendatore or The City and Its Uncertain Walls and want to know what Japan made of them
  • You follow the Nobel Prize every October and want to know what the odds can and cannot tell you
  • You want a critic who takes a position and leaves room for you to disagree

What's inside:

  • Why Colorless Tsukuru Tazaki ends with a man falling asleep, and what that says about his return to realism
  • The Nanjing passage in Killing Commendatore, who attacked it, who defended it, and a critic's reading of the novel as a portrait of nothing
  • One walled town, three attempts: a 1980 novella he called a failure, Hard-Boiled Wonderland in 1985, and the 2023 novel
  • The Nobel Prize, the sealed nomination records, and what bookmakers' odds actually measure
  • How Hamaguchi built Drive My Car from three Murakami stories, in his own words
  • "Murakami Studies": a library, a symposium with bus tours, and tourists walking his old neighborhood

Built from primary sources:

Murakami's Japanese texts, interviews, critics' essays, and press coverage from Japan and abroad. Key Japanese passages appear in English, with the original alongside.

About the author

Tyler Atsumori was born and raised in Kyoto to an American mother and a Japanese father, studied modern and contemporary Japanese literature in the Kansai region, and reads the Japanese-language criticism and recent scholarship that English-language writing on Murakami tends to skip. His morning jog is serious business in Kyoto, where summers keep getting hotter and the humidity in the mountain-ringed basin is said to be among the highest in Japan. When his lungs reach their limit, he repeats a mantra Murakami once borrowed from a newspaper article on marathon runners: "Pain is inevitable. Suffering is optional."

This is Volume Three, the last, of Murakami in Three Acts. Volume One, Murakami, Untranslated (1979–1987), and Volume Two, Murakami, Everywhere (1988–2010), are also available.
