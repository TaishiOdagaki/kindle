In 1979 and again in 1980, Haruki Murakami's first two novels were nominated for the Akutagawa Prize — Japan's most prestigious award for new writers. Both times, the jury passed. Several of the jurors who weighed in questioned whether his prose even sounded like Japanese literature. By 1985, the same writer was winning the Tanizaki Prize, one of the country's senior literary honors, for Hard-Boiled Wonderland and the End of the World. So what changed in between, and what never did?

The "foreign" voice the early judges distrusted was no accident. Murakami has said he drafted his debut novel in English first, then hand-translated it back into Japanese. This book follows that technique and the decade of domestic doubt and uneasy triumph that came after: from a spring afternoon at a Tokyo ballpark in 1978, through the bestseller that made him impossible to ignore at home, and sent him running to Greece and Italy instead of enjoying it.

This book is for you if:

  • You love Murakami and want the argument underneath the reputation
  • You have never read a word of him and want to know what the fuss is about
  • The Nobel Prize conversation has you wondering where his story actually started
  • You read him in English and wonder what translation changed

What's inside:

  • The real story behind Murakami's "foreign" voice: a debut novel drafted in English, then hand-translated back into Japanese.
  • The actual 1979 and 1980 Akutagawa Prize jury remarks, sourced to the original prize records, plus a popular myth corrected on the page: what Kenzaburō Ōe is widely believed to have said about Murakami's debut, versus what he is actually on record saying
  • A close reading of the "Trilogy of the Rat" and the split narration of Hard-Boiled Wonderland and the End of the World: one mind, two pronouns, two entirely different registers, quoted from both the original Japanese and the published English translations
  • Why Norwegian Wood selling over ten million copies in Japan alone sent its own author further from home, not closer to it
  • Nine chapters, ending in a verdict that takes a position on what "the Murakami voice" really is, plus a glossary and full sources so you can check every claim

Why trust it:

Every specific claim, date, and quotation in this book was checked against a primary source before it was written down: original prize-jury remarks, first-edition Japanese texts, and published English translations, not secondhand summaries of them.

About the author

Tyler Atsumori was born and raised in Kyoto to an American mother and a Japanese father, and studied modern and contemporary Japanese literature at a university in the Kansai region. A graduate reading group's slow work through the postwar canon became a longer project: tracking how Japanese novelists are read and argued about at home, versus how they are received once translated abroad. That bilingual background shapes this book. It draws on Japanese-language sources most English-language criticism never reaches: literary-magazine criticism, prize-jury commentary, and Murakami's own essays.

This is Volume One of a planned three-volume series. Volume Two, Murakami, Everywhere (1988–2010), is coming soon.
