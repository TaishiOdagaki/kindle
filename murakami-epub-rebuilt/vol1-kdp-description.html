In April 1978, a jazz-bar owner lay down on the grass at a Tokyo baseball game, watched a Texan hit a double, and decided to write a novel. Japan's most serious literary judges later said, in print, that the result sounded foreign. They weren't wrong, and that was the point.

Haruki Murakami has said he wrote the first chapter of his debut novel in English, a language he barely knew, then translated it back into Japanese himself. This book follows what that trick built: a flat, quiet, faintly foreign voice that bothered the judges at Japan's top prize for new writers, carried one novel past ten million copies at home, and sent its author running to Greece and Italy to get away from the attention. It is also the story of his fans, who hold Nobel watch parties and bring party poppers, and of the critics who were not impressed at first.

This book is for you if:

  • You love Murakami and want to know where that voice came from
  • You have never read him and wonder what the fuss is about
  • You follow the Nobel Prize every October and wonder how his story started
  • You read him in translation and are curious what Japanese readers and critics heard

What's inside:

  • The real jury remarks from 1979 and 1980, translated from the original Japanese, including a popular story about who said what that turns out to be wrong
  • How the English-first draft shaped his sentences, with passages in Japanese and in English translation
  • Why "sounds like a translation" was a serious insult in Japanese literary circles
  • The Rat trilogy, and the split narration of Hard-Boiled Wonderland and the End of the World: one mind, two voices
  • Norwegian Wood, ten million copies, and why the biggest success of his career left him feeling disliked
  • A verdict that takes a position, and leaves you room to argue with it

Built from primary sources:

Prize-jury records, first-edition Japanese texts, Murakami's own essays, and the published English translations. Quotations from Japanese sources appear in English, with the original alongside.

About the author

Tyler Atsumori was born and raised in Kyoto to an American mother and a Japanese father, studied modern and contemporary Japanese literature in the Kansai region, and reads the Japanese-language criticism that English-language writing on Murakami tends to skip. Every morning he runs with Murakami's novels playing in his ears, in Japanese, on audiobook. Sometimes he listens harder than he runs, and has put a brand-new pair of Asics straight into a puddle.

This is Volume One of a planned three-volume series. Volume Two, Murakami, Everywhere (1988–2010), is coming soon.
