In 1979 and again in 1980, Haruki Murakami's first two novels were nominated for the Akutagawa Prize — Japan's most prestigious award for new writers. Both times, the jury passed. Several of the jurors who weighed in questioned whether his prose even sounded like Japanese literature. By 1985, the same writer was winning the Tanizaki Prize, one of the country's senior literary honors, for Hard-Boiled Wonderland and the End of the World. This book is about what changed in between, and what did not.

The "foreign" voice the early judges distrusted was no accident. Murakami has said he drafted his debut novel in English first, then hand-translated it back into Japanese. This book traces that technique and the decade of domestic doubt and uneasy triumph that followed: from a spring afternoon at a Tokyo ballpark in 1978, through the sales phenomenon that finally made him inescapable at home, and sent him running to Greece and Italy instead of enjoying it. Written by a bilingual researcher who works from the original Japanese-language jury records, criticism, and essays that most English-language coverage never reaches.

What's inside:

  • The story behind Murakami's "foreign" voice: drafting his debut novel in English, then hand-translating it back into Japanese. Every chapter builds on this technique.
  • The actual 1979 and 1980 Akutagawa Prize jury remarks, sourced to the original prize records, including a popular myth this book corrects on the page: what Kenzaburō Ōe is widely believed to have said about Murakami's debut, versus what he is actually on record saying
  • A close reading of the "Trilogy of the Rat" and the split narration of Hard-Boiled Wonderland and the End of the World: one mind, two pronouns, two entirely different registers, quoted from both the original Japanese and the published English translations
  • Why Norwegian Wood becoming an unignorable, ten-million-copy phenomenon in Japan sent its own author further from home, not closer to it
  • A verdict chapter that takes a position on what "the Murakami voice" really is, rather than surveying everyone else's opinion

Every specific claim, date, and quotation in this book was checked against a primary source before it was written down: original prize-jury remarks, first-edition Japanese texts, and published English translations, not secondhand summaries of them.
