# MURAKAMI, UNFINISHED

### 2013–2025: The Late Novels, the Nobel Bet, and Still Being Read as an Outsider

*A Japanese Culture Press Book*

---

When the producer of *Drive My Car* first asked Ryūsuke Hamaguchi to adapt a Murakami story, he proposed a different one. The producer, Akihisa Yamamoto, is someone Hamaguchi calls practically a Harukist. Hamaguchi thought it over and found that story too hard. Then he remembered "Drive My Car," a story in which only real things happen. No otherworld. For a film without an enormous budget, that mattered.[^brutus-hook]

But Hamaguchi knew what the choice left out. Murakami's charm, he says, especially in the long novels, is the feeling of digging a well down through reality into a world underneath. So he borrowed motifs from two other stories in the same collection, "Scheherazade" and "Kino," to bring some of that depth back. The film that won the Oscar for Best International Feature in 2022 is three Murakami stories braided together and held in place by a red car.

That is the Murakami of 2013 to 2025, the years this volume covers. He is big enough that a film director has to decide how much of him can be put on a screen, and he is still going back to the same walled town. This book asks whether there is anything left for a writer this large to risk. You do not need the earlier volumes to follow it.

[^brutus-hook]: *BRUTUS* (brutus.jp), 「『ドライブ・マイ・カー』映画監督・濱口竜介にインタビュー。村上春樹の小説を映画化する時に最も困難なこと」, February 25, 2022; Hamaguchi's account of how the project began and why the film borrows from "Scheherazade" and "Kino" is paraphrased from that interview (see Chapter 1).

## About the Author

Tyler Atsumori was born and raised in Kyoto to an American mother and a Japanese father, studied modern and contemporary Japanese literature at a university in the Kansai region, and reads the Japanese-language criticism and recent scholarship that English-language writing on Murakami tends to skip. His morning jog is serious business in Kyoto, where summers keep getting hotter and the humidity in the mountain-ringed basin is said to be among the highest in Japan. When his lungs reach their limit, he repeats a mantra Murakami once borrowed from a newspaper article on marathon runners: "Pain is inevitable. Suffering is optional."

## CHAPTER 1: THREE STORIES AND A RED CAR

**An Oscar for a Short Story**

The biggest jump in Murakami's international visibility this century did not come from a novel. It came from a three-hour film based on stories from his 2014 collection *Men Without Women* (女のいない男たち). *Drive My Car*, directed by Ryūsuke Hamaguchi and co-written with Takamasa Oe, premiered in 2021 and went on to a run at the 94th Academy Awards that no Japanese film had managed before: nominations for Best Picture and Best International Feature Film, the first time a Japanese production had been nominated for Best Picture, along with nods for Best Director and Best Adapted Screenplay. It won Best International Feature, only the second Japanese film to do so competitively, after *Departures* in 2009. Months earlier, at Cannes, it had won the festival's Best Screenplay prize, the first time a Japanese screenwriter had won it.[^12]

**A Story He Had Carried Since His Thirties**

Hamaguchi first read "Drive My Car" in his mid-thirties, in its original magazine appearance in *Bungeishunjū*, on someone's recommendation.[^brutus] What struck him was how close it ran to his own work: a story about acting, told largely through conversations in a car. Filming Murakami felt unrealistic to him then, he says, but he remembers thinking that this might be one he could approach.

**Why Three Stories**

Filmed as written, the story would not have given a feature-length audience a clear resolution, Hamaguchi says, and that would have been frustrating. He found the answer in the same collection. Rereading *Men Without Women*, he realized that "Kino" already held it. The bar where "Kino" takes place even appears in "Drive My Car." Hamaguchi read "Kino" as the place where something had changed in Murakami himself while he wrote the collection, and made it the place where his protagonist, Kafuku, ends up. By the time he wrote to ask Murakami's permission, he says, the film's structure was already close to the one that reached the screen.

"Scheherazade" did other work. It fills out Kafuku's wife, and it supplied what she leaves behind: stories, tied up with sex, that the younger actor Takatsuki carries.

**What Was Already in the Story**

The Chekhov was not an addition. In Murakami's story Kafuku is an actor who plays Vanya in *Uncle Vanya*, and the story already ties Kafuku to Vanya, and the young driver Misaki to Vanya's niece Sonya. Hamaguchi points to Sonya's lament about being born so plain, which Misaki takes as her own, and to Vanya's arithmetic of the years he still has to live if he dies at sixty, which Kafuku hears as his. The film widened what the story had planted.

Misaki grew for another reason. Hamaguchi calls her one of the most attractive characters Murakami has written, and wanting to keep her mystery intact led him to what he does with every actor: writing the character a backstory. That backstory swelled, and on revision it flowed back into the film itself. One influence he names is a book Volume Two of this series has already used, *Murakami Haruki, Kawai Hayao ni Ai ni Iku*, the dialogues in which Murakami and the psychologist Hayao Kawai talk about stories as never cleanly separate from reality, a shape in which repressed material from the unconscious appears.

**Small Changes, Large Reasons**

The smaller changes had reasons too. Kafuku's yellow Saab 900 convertible became a red Saab 900 Turbo with a sunroof, because the landscape is mostly green and red stands out against it. In the story Kafuku sits in the passenger seat. In the film he sits mostly in the back, because a passenger in the back is less in the driver's line of sight and so more at ease, and the passenger seat, which suggests a closer relationship, could be saved for later. One plot detail changed outright: in the story a key character's mother dies in a driving accident, and in the film she dies in a landslide.

**The Speech He Left Alone**

One passage hardly changed. In the long speech where Takatsuki tells Kafuku that anyone who truly wants to see another person has no choice but to look deeply and straight at himself, the film follows the story almost word for word. It was the part that stayed clearest for Hamaguchi when he first read it. In the story, Kafuku hears the words as rising from some deep, special place in Takatsuki, and Hamaguchi thinks Murakami wrote them by diving to a place like that himself. The words carry an unclouded ring, he says, and a line like that works on the actor who has to say it. He gives the credit to Masaki Okada, who plays Takatsuki. The film moves the speech from a bar to the car, so that Misaki hears it too, as the ending requires, and so that it sits with the title.

**What He Left Out on Purpose**

Asked what makes Murakami hard to film, Hamaguchi points to the otherworldly parts. The trouble is that Murakami's prose has a realism of description that makes you believe something that does not exist surely does. Adding fantasy to the screen, he says, will never reach the strength of conviction that it is all happening inside our own hearts.

So the film that won the Oscar is a version of Murakami's realist side, with some of the depth borrowed back from two other stories. In a volume about a writer who keeps returning to the same walled town, it shows what a careful reader could carry out of Murakami's world and what he chose to leave behind.

## CHAPTER 2: THE RETURN TO REALISM, AGAIN

**A Plainer Book Than Expected**

After *1Q84*'s two alternate Tokyos, its cult, its rewritten fairy tale, and its two moons in one sky, readers had reason to expect Murakami's next novel to go even further out. It went the other way entirely. *Colorless Tsukuru Tazaki and His Years of Pilgrimage* (色彩を持たない多崎つくると、彼の巡礼の年), Murakami's thirteenth novel, published on April 12, 2013,[^1] is a contained, grounded, unusually readable story about a man confronting, decades later, why four childhood friends cut him off without explanation. Critics reviewing it at the time noted, with some surprise, just how firmly the book was rooted in realism, a comparatively straightforward "Murakami world" next to the genre-bending architecture of *1Q84* or *Kafka on the Shore*.

**The Same Register, a Different Kind of Loss**

That description should sound familiar by now. It's close to the exact language critics reached for describing *Norwegian Wood* in 1987, and the parallel is real. Both are relatively short, tightly focused novels about a protagonist working through a specific, unresolved loss from his own past, told in a stripped-down realist mode that stands out precisely because it's surrounded, on both sides of the timeline, by Murakami's more surreal work. But critics have also pointed to a real difference in what each book does with its loss. *Norwegian Wood* is a story that keeps losing, an accumulation of deaths and absences the narrator never quite recovers from by the book's own close. *Colorless Tsukuru Tazaki*, by contrast, has been read as a story of recovery: the same basic architecture, wound and unresolved grief triggering a first-person reckoning, but built this time toward something closer to repair.

**A Tool, Not a Statement, By 2013**

What does it mean that a writer would go back to the same realist register twice, roughly a quarter-century apart, at two very different points in his own career? In 1987, the turn to realism was a deliberate departure, a "100% realism novel" he wrote specifically to signal distance from the surreal registers of *A Wild Sheep Chase* and *Hard-Boiled Wonderland*, as Volume One of this series covered in detail. By 2013, the same move reads less like a departure from anything and more like a tool he now has available, one register among several rather than a statement about which kind of writer he's trying to prove himself to be. A writer only gets to make that kind of move without it looking like retreat once he's spent enough decades proving he can do the other thing too.

**A Blankness That, For Once, Doesn't Hurt**

Not every quiet moment in the novel is grief. Earlier on, Sunday mornings at the university pool with his college friend Haida give Tsukuru's characteristic blankness a version that does not hurt: 「灰田の柔らかなキックが小さな白い泡をリズミカルに水中につくり出す光景を、つくるは無心に眺めていた。その光景は彼に常に軽い意識の麻痺状態をもたらした」 In English, roughly: Tsukuru would watch, mind empty, the sight of Haida's soft kick stirring small white bubbles rhythmically through the water; the sight always brought him a light paralysis of consciousness.[^2] Empty, 空っぽ, is the word the novel keeps reaching for to describe Tsukuru's inner life. Here, for half an hour a week, the same emptiness is no wound at all, only rhythm.

**Twelve Rings, and a Light Going Out**

The novel's closing pages make the recovery-not-resolution distinction literal rather than just structural. Tsukuru has told Sara he wants an answer, whether she'll stay with him or go to the other man she's been seeing, and she's asked for three days to think it over. The phone rings while he waits, and twice he lets it ring itself out instead of picking up: 「コールが十二回続くあいだ、つくるは迷っていた」, twelve rings, while Tsukuru wavered.[^3] He doesn't resolve the wavering. He goes back to the Lazar Berman recording of Liszt's *Années de pèlerinage* he'd put on before the first call, the piece this novel's own Japanese title borrows its "pilgrimage" from, and lets the second call ring out without even counting.

The book's actual last words don't give Tsukuru, or the reader, Sara's answer at all. He goes to bed still not knowing, and the final paragraph describes nothing more than him falling asleep: 「彼は心を静め、目を閉じて眠りについた。意識の最後尾の明かりが、遠ざかっていく最終の特急列車のように、徐々にスピードを増しながら小さくなり、あとには白樺の木立を抜ける風の音だけが残った」 In English, roughly: he calmed himself, closed his eyes, and fell asleep; the last light of his consciousness receded like a departing night express, picking up speed as it shrank, until all that remained was the sound of wind moving through a stand of white birch trees.[^3] No answer from Sara, no decision from Tsukuru: a man asleep, and wind through trees. If *Norwegian Wood* closes on a narrator who still doesn't know where he is, *Colorless Tsukuru Tazaki* closes on one who's stopped needing an answer before he can rest. That is the shape of the recovery this chapter has been pointing to: a wound its owner can finally sleep on without resolving it first.

## CHAPTER 3: PAINTING OVER THE PAST

*A note on this chapter: it discusses the Nanjing Massacre, a real historical atrocity that mainstream historians do not seriously dispute occurred, though the precise casualty figures remain a genuine subject of academic debate. The treatment here is sourced to multiple outlets and written with care given how reputationally and politically sensitive this category of claim is.*

**A Phone Call About Nanjing**

*Killing Commendatore* (騎士団長殺し), published in Japan on February 24, 2017 with an initial print run of 1.3 million copies,[^4] is a long, formally strange novel about a portrait painter and a mysterious painting come partially to life. One thread in it became a political controversy. A wealthy, enigmatic neighbor named Menshiki (免色) explains to the narrator, over the phone, the family history of the painter Tomohiko Amada, whose younger brother Tsuguhiko was conscripted, forced into direct participation in atrocities at Nanjing, and took his own life after his discharge, unable to live with what he'd done. Menshiki states the historical fact directly. In the original Japanese: 「正確に何人が殺害されたか、細部については歴史学者のあいだにも異論がありますが、とにかくおびただしい数の市民が戦闘の巻き添えになって殺されたことは、打ち消しがたい事実です。中国人死者の数を四十万人というものもいれば、十万人というものもいます。」 In English, roughly: exactly how many were killed is disputed even among historians, but that an enormous number of citizens were killed, caught up in the fighting, is an undeniable fact regardless; some put the number of Chinese dead at 400,000, others at 100,000.[^5]

The passage names competing estimates and insists on the killing itself as settled fact, whichever count is used. That the massacre occurred is not a matter of serious dispute among mainstream historians in Japan or internationally. What remains academically contested is the death toll, with published estimates depending on methodology and on which killings and time period are counted. Both figures the novel cites sit within that real range. Refusing to let the reader hide behind uncertainty over the exact count was never going to read as neutral history in Japan in 2017.

**Reactions Followed Predictable Lines**

The Nanjing Massacre Memorial Hall in China responded on its official Weibo account with approval, quoting Murakami's own "no difference between 400,000 and 100,000" framing and noting that the postwar Nanjing War Crimes Tribunal had found more than 300,000 victims,[^6] and some Chinese commentators online praised him as a writer of conscience for naming the event at all. In Japan the passage drew direct public criticism from the novelist Naoki Hyakuta and from Makoto Sakurai, the former leader of the ultranationalist group Zaitokukai (see the glossary), with the conservative *Sankei Shimbun* also weighing in critically.[^7] Hyakuta went further and suggested that Murakami was angling for Chinese goodwill, and by extension Nobel Prize momentum, by including it. Sakurai quoted the passage's substance back at his followers, close enough to the line about the Japanese military lacking the capacity to manage prisoners that his objection was plainly to the specific claim, not to a vague sense that the book had gone political. Critics answering back, including the outlet *Lite-Ra*, argued that Hyakuta and Sakurai had missed a larger current in the novel: a sustained argument against historical revisionism, not an isolated provocation.[^7] Contemporary coverage described mainstream Japanese news portals as carrying a substantial volume of direct criticism of the novel over the passage, not silence.

**A Novel Built Out of Portraits It Can't Paint**

The critic Sasaki Atsushi has laid out a larger pattern behind the passage: *Killing Commendatore* is a novel about a portrait painter that is itself, structurally, a portrait, specifically of the one thing its narrator insists he cannot paint.[^8] The unnamed narrator, thirty-six and freshly separated from his wife, moves into the mountain home of the elderly, now-institutionalized Tomohiko Amada and over the following months paints several portraits: of the wealthy neighbor Menshiki, of a teenage girl named Akikawa Marie, of a recurring, threatening figure he calls "the Man with the White Subaru Forester." What he never manages to paint is a literally faceless man who visits him twice, at the beginning and the end, asking for exactly that. The narrator's own explanation is as plain as the problem: *"There's simply nothing there. How do you give form to nothing?"*[^8] By Sasaki's reading, that unpaintable "nothing" is the real subject of the book the narrator says, on its own first pages, that he is writing. Not a painting this time, but the six-hundred-page novel itself, built as a substitute portrait of the one blank his own medium couldn't hold.

Sasaki also reconstructs a second layer of history behind the hidden painting that gives the novel its name: a young Tomohiko in Vienna before the 1938 Anschluss, drawn into a resistance plot and repatriated by his father before it could be carried out, with the co-conspirators he left behind arrested and very likely killed, while his younger brother Tsuguhiko was drafted, sent to China, and, by Sasaki's account, present at the fall of Nanjing in December 1937. Sasaki is explicit that this rests on inference. As he puts it, 「らしい」 ("it seems") is doing a lot of work here, "because everything stays within the realm of speculation to the very end."[^8]

**The Clock Behind the Novel**

For most of its length, *Killing Commendatore* never says what year it is. That changes in the final chapter, when the narrator, years after the fact, mentions watching television coverage of "a large earthquake" that struck the Tōhoku coast on March 11, devastating, among other places, one of the small coastal towns he'd once passed through, followed two months later by a fire that destroyed the mountain house at the center of the story.[^8] Working backward from a detail in that same chapter, Akikawa Marie's age at a later point, Sasaki calculates that the central events, the ones the narrator spends six hundred pages recounting, take place from May 2007 to early 2008: a story set three to four years before the disaster it mentions only on its last pages.

Murakami has built a book this way before. Volume Two covered *after the quake* (神の子どもたちはみな踊る), the 2000 collection whose six stories are all set in February 1995, in the narrow gap between the Great Hanshin earthquake in January and the Aum Shinrikyo sarin attack in March, without depicting either on the page.[^8] *Killing Commendatore* runs a longer version of the same maneuver: a whole novel of private, surreal, metaphor-laden crisis that turns out, only at the end, to have been counting down to March 11, 2011, a disaster the book never describes and that its narrator only watches afterward, on television, from a distance. Sasaki does not read the earthquake and the Fukushima disaster as the novel's subject in any literal sense. He reads Murakami as a writer who, faced with an event his fiction could not approach any more directly than his narrator could paint a faceless man, built a long, self-referential novel about the impossibility of portraying "nothing" and then quietly set its last page on the day that nothing arrived.

**Placed Against the Rest of This Series**

Set this against what the earlier volumes traced. A writer whose 1979 debut was dismissed as too imitative of foreign fiction, who spent the 1990s proving through the Nomonhan material of *The Wind-Up Bird Chronicle* that his surreal register could hold real historical weight, and who worked out in *after the quake* an oblique way of approaching disaster sideways, built a character's family tragedy around a historical fact that a meaningful share of his domestic readership still found difficult to hear stated at all. On one plausible critical reading, he then used the novel's whole architecture to approach a second, more recent catastrophe he had still not found a direct way to write about. Writing that down and publishing it under his own name, at that moment, in that country, was no small thing. At nearly seventy, and decades past his own literary establishment's harshest early verdicts, he did it anyway, on more than one register at once.

## CHAPTER 4: THE NOBEL BET THAT NEVER PAYS OFF

**An Annual Ritual**

Every October, for the better part of two decades now, some section of the Japanese press has run some version of the same story: Murakami is a leading candidate for the Nobel Prize in Literature, the announcement is imminent, this could finally be the year. Every October, the Swedish Academy names someone else, and the cycle resets for the following autumn. It's become such a reliable annual ritual that "Murakami, snubbed again" is practically its own minor journalistic genre at this point.

**What the Sealed Records Actually Say**

Here's the detail that genre of coverage tends to skip past: the Nobel Prize in Literature doesn't publish its shortlist. Nominees are kept sealed for fifty years under the prize's own rules, occasionally surfacing only long after the fact.[^11] There is no public, official list confirming Murakami has ever actually been a Nobel finalist in any specific year, because that information simply isn't released while any candidate is alive to read it. What actually exists, and what actually drives the annual headlines, is something considerably less authoritative: British bookmakers running ordinary betting markets on the prize, the same kind of speculative odds-setting they'd apply to a horse race or an election, with Murakami's name a fixture near the top of that market for over a decade, at odds that have swung considerably from year to year, from as short as 3 to 1 to as long as 16 to 1 depending on the bookmaker and the season.[^11] Japanese media coverage has spent two decades treating those betting odds as though they were a genuine signal from inside the Swedish Academy's actual deliberations, when they're really just a reflection of which names casual bettors, and the oddsmakers pricing their bets, recognize.

**Betting Odds Aren't a Signal From Stockholm**

None of which means the underlying question, why hasn't he won, isn't worth asking on its own terms, separate from the bookmaker theater built up around it. One recurring explanation among Japanese commentators who've tracked the prize closely points to changes in the Swedish Academy's own membership over the years, and the corresponding shift in the kind of writer and the kind of work that membership has tended to recognize. That's a real, structural, institutional explanation, and a considerably less satisfying one than "he keeps almost winning," because it suggests the more accurate framing was never about Murakami's own work falling just short year after year. It's that an entire domestic media cycle built a two-decade-long "will he finally win" narrative on top of a betting market that was never actually reporting anything about the prize at all.

[^11]: The Nobel Prize's nomination records are sealed for fifty years under its own rules; as of this writing, only nominations submitted through 1975 have been publicly released. Kōbō Abe, who died in 1993, was widely regarded at the time as one of the Japanese writers closest to winning the prize, though the nomination records confirming any specific consideration remain sealed until decades after this book's publication. On the betting markets themselves: British bookmaker Ladbrokes has listed Murakami among the favorites in most years since at least 2011, at odds that have ranged from roughly 16 to 1 (2011) to 3 to 1 (2013) to 14 to 1 (2024), among other years in between — a spread wide enough on its own to show the odds are tracking public speculation, not any actual signal from the Swedish Academy.

## CHAPTER 5: THE BOOK HE WENT BACK TO FINISH, TWICE

**An Origin Story Longer Than the Book Itself**

*The City and Its Uncertain Walls* (街とその不確かな壁), published April 13, 2023, is a roughly 1,200-manuscript-page novel[^9] with an origin story considerably longer than the book itself. It starts in 1980, when Murakami published a novella called *The Town, and Its Uncertain Wall* (街と、その不確かな壁, note the comma his later title drops), his own account of which has never been kind: he's described the piece as published in an unsatisfying, half-finished shape, and has said outright, in later interviews, that the novella was "a failure," that he "shouldn't have written it" in the form it took. That's an unusually harsh verdict for a writer to pass on his own published work, and it deserves to be taken seriously rather than read as false modesty.

**A Second Pass, Still Unsettled**

Readers of Volume One of this series already know where the next part of this story goes, even if they don't know it yet. In 1985, dissatisfied with how that 1980 novella had turned out, Murakami reworked its core material into *Hard-Boiled Wonderland and the End of the World*, the same novel this series' first volume examined closely for its split *watashi*/*boku* narration, the walled, seasonless Town guarded by golden beasts belonging specifically to the *boku* half of that book's divided narrator. Even that second pass did not settle the matter for him. He's since said he felt he'd rushed it, that he wished he'd let the material sit for another two years before publishing again. The dissatisfaction that started with a 1980 novella he called a failure survived one full novel built to work through it and still wasn't resolved.

**What Carried Over, and What Got Built Out**

Set the 2023 novel's Town next to the one Volume One quoted from *Hard-Boiled Wonderland*, and the continuity is immediate, down to the phrasing. The golden beasts are still there, still turning gold on the same seasonal schedule: 「秋、獣たちの体は、来たるべき寒い季節に備えて輝かしい金色の毛に覆われる。額に生えた単角は鋭く白い」 In English, roughly: in autumn, their bodies are covered in brilliant golden fur in preparation for the coming cold season, the single horn on their forehead sharp and white.[^10] It's the same image Alfred Birnbaum's translation of *Hard-Boiled Wonderland* rendered, decades earlier, as golden fur that comes into the world "as gold" and "exists in this world as gold". This is the same animal, described the same way, thirty-eight years apart.

What's different is how much more the 2023 version builds around that image. The gate itself now has a full-time keeper: a physically imposing, dutiful man who shaves his own head and face every morning with the same large, sharp razor he uses to groom the beasts, and who climbs a small tower by the gatehouse morning and evening to sound a horn, a soft, resonant sound the narrator marvels at, given how rough the man himself looks.[^10] The beasts' world outside the wall gets its own, considerably darker season: for one week each spring, danger to the town aside, they fight each other for mates with their horns aimed at each other's throats and bellies, "forgetting even to eat," fighting to the death in numbers, and the Gatekeeper closes the gate entirely for that week so the violence never reaches the streets.[^10] None of that mating-season brutality is part of the version of the Town Volume One traced through *Hard-Boiled Wonderland*'s "Golden Beasts" chapter. The 2023 book keeps the core image intact and then spends four hundred more pages than either earlier attempt building out the mechanics, rituals, and violence the walled Town runs on underneath it.

**Unfinished Business in the Most Literal Sense**

The 2023 novel is the third attempt at the same underlying material, more than four decades after the first one, and the persistence says something. This is not a writer returning to old material because he has run out of new ideas this late in his career; everything else in this volume argues the opposite, a writer still willing to take on real historical risk and real formal experiment well into his seventies. It's something closer to unfinished business in the most literal sense available to a novelist: a walled town he first imagined at thirty-one, rebuilt at thirty-six inside one of his most acclaimed early novels, and still didn't feel he'd gotten right until he came back a third time, at seventy-four, to finish it properly. Few writers get handed the chance to revise the same founding image of their own career three separate times, decades apart, in full public view. Fewer still would have the nerve to call the first two attempts failures on the way to the third.

[^9]: *The City and Its Uncertain Walls* [街とその不確かな壁] was published by Shinchōsha on April 13, 2023. Murakami began writing it in March 2020, completing the roughly 1,200-manuscript-page novel over approximately three years.
[^10]: Murakami Haruki, *The City and Its Uncertain Walls* [街とその不確かな壁] (Tokyo: Shinchōsha, 2023); the golden beasts, the Gatekeeper's razor and horn, and the spring mating-week violence all quoted directly from the author's copy.

## CHAPTER 6: MURAKAMI STUDIES, ABROAD

**An Academic Field of Its Own**

By the 2020s, "Murakami" had become an academic field in its own right, not just a body of work critics wrote occasional essays about. Waseda University, his own alma mater, houses the Waseda International House of Literature, publicly identified alongside its own name as the Haruki Murakami Library, built specifically to hold his manuscripts and archives and to host ongoing academic programming. The International Murakami Haruki Symposium, a recurring scholarly conference dedicated entirely to his work and organized in recent years by Tamkang University's Murakami Haruki Research Center, had reached its fourteenth iteration by the time it convened at Kyoto University in July 2025,[^13] complete with organized bus tours as part of its own programming, the kind of institutional infrastructure usually reserved for writers who've been dead long enough to enter the settled academic canon. A separate East Asia Murakami Haruki Research Association runs its own parallel track of scholarship and cross-border symposia.

**Two Different Audiences, Two Continents**

The geography of that attention splits. Academic symposium attendance skews heavily European, drawing scholars from across the continent to conferences held in Japan. Commercial readership, by contrast, runs strongest across East Asia and its Chinese-speaking communities specifically, China, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Malaysia, alongside the broader global sales this series has already tracked. The people studying Murakami in university settings and the people buying and reading him in the largest numbers are, to a meaningful degree, two different audiences, on two different continents, doing two different kinds of engaging with the same body of work.

**A Third Kind of Engagement: The Street Level**

And underneath both of those, at street level, is a third kind of engagement entirely: literary tourism. Guided walking tours through Tokyo trace specific locations tied to his fiction and his own biography, with Waseda University treated as something close to an origin point, the place a young, not-yet-published Murakami studied before he ever opened Peter Cat or sat down to write *Hear the Wind Sing* in the small hours after closing the bar. A writer whose own country's literary establishment spent 1979 and 1980 questioning whether his prose belonged to any recognizable Japanese tradition now has a university-sponsored library holding his manuscripts, a recurring international academic conference in its double-digit iteration, and tourists walking his old neighborhood specifically because he once lived and worked there. Institutional legitimacy of exactly the kind the Akutagawa jury declined to grant him arrived eventually, just by a route nobody on that jury could have predicted: not through the prize itself, but around it, built up by decades of readers and scholars who never needed the Akutagawa's permission to take him seriously in the first place.

[^13]: The 14th International Murakami Haruki Symposium was held July 5–6, 2025, at Kyoto University, organized by Tamkang University's Murakami Haruki Research Center. The Haruki Murakami Library is the public name paired with Waseda University's International House of Literature.

## CHAPTER 7: THE OUTSIDER WHO NEVER STOPPED BEING ONE

**The Status That Kept Relocating**

This volume opened by asking whether there was anything left for a writer this large, this studied, this translated, to risk, or whether "Murakami" had simply become too big a name to ever sound like an outsider again. Chapter by chapter, the answer turned out to be that the outsider status never went away. It kept relocating.

He returned, twice now, to the realist register he first used to signal a deliberate departure from what people expected of him, and each time it read less like a retreat than like a writer reminding his own audience he was never only one kind of novelist. He wrote a single sentence naming a historical atrocity that a meaningful share of his own country still isn't willing to hear stated plainly, and wore the resulting backlash from named public figures rather than walking the line back. He went back to material whose first version he'd bluntly called a failure and whose second version he'd never fully stopped feeling he'd rushed, at an age when most novelists with his reputation would have stopped taking that particular risk, and published a third attempt anyway. And the whole time, an entire domestic press corps built a two-decade fable around his supposed nearness to a prize whose actual shortlist no one outside a sealed Swedish archive has ever been allowed to see.

**Not the Profile of a Writer Fully Absorbed Into Safety**

None of that is the profile of a writer who's been fully absorbed into safety. A writer that safe doesn't publish a passage that gets him denounced by name in his own country's press. He doesn't spend his seventies rewriting his own earliest, most personally damning verdict on his own work rather than letting it stand. The specific shape of the outsider status changed completely across the four and a half decades this series has covered: from a debut novelist accused of sounding too foreign for serious Japanese literature, to a writer whose own historical honesty made him a target for a different kind of domestic hostility, to a man in his seventies still dissatisfied enough with his own oldest unfinished business to go back and fix it in public. What stayed constant, underneath every one of those different shapes, is a writer who never fully settled into being comfortable, at home, in his own country's literary establishment, even once that establishment's opinion had stopped being able to slow him down at all.

## CHAPTER 8: VERDICT: SERIES CONCLUSION

**A Narrower Question, Answered Early**

This series opened with a narrower question than the one it closes on now: was the flat, faintly foreign-sounding voice English-language readers know as "Murakami" something his translators built, or something he built himself. The answer, established early and never seriously complicated since, was that he built it, deliberately, by routing his own debut novel through English and back, discovering a plainer Japanese by way of a language he barely knew. Everything this series has traced since then, the split pronouns of *Hard-Boiled Wonderland*, the deliberate realist departures of *Norwegian Wood* and *Colorless Tsukuru Tazaki*, the historical weight of *The Wind-Up Bird Chronicle* and *Killing Commendatore*, the scheduled national event of *1Q84*, the third attempt at a walled town he first imagined at thirty-one, has been a record of what that one early technique turned into once a writer built out of not-quite-belonging kept being read, at home, as someone who still didn't quite belong.

**The Engine, and the Origin Story**

That is the throughline connecting an origin story about prose technique to a career this large and this argued-over: the not-quite-belonging was more than a stylistic accident that happened to draw institutional suspicion in 1979. It was the engine. It's what let a writer accused of reading too much foreign fiction eventually succeed at historical material a revered domestic novelist had spent a decade researching and abandoned as too bleak to touch. It's what let him state a plain historical fact his own country still found difficult to hear, and survive the backlash as one more entry in a career built around not needing everyone's approval at once. It's what let him look at his own most personally damning verdict, on his own earliest published work, and decide that wasn't the end of the story either.

**What the Reputation Was**

English-language readers who fell for the flat, displaced voice of the translated Murakami were responding, from the very beginning, to a writer whose relationship to his own country, his own language, and his own literary tradition had never been fully settled, and never would be. That was never a flaw in the reputation. Across four and a half decades, three national traumas, two attempts at the same unfinished story turned into three, and one prize he was never confirmed to have been up for, it was the entire reputation.

## GLOSSARY

- **The 400,000 / 100,000 figures** — The two competing casualty estimates for the Nanjing Massacre that *Killing Commendatore* names side by side, in a passage arguing the precise gap between them matters less than the underlying historical fact. Both sit within a genuine, disputed academic range; the massacre's occurrence itself is not seriously disputed by mainstream historians. See Chapter 3.
- **The "portrait of nothing"** — The critic Sasaki Atsushi's term for the recurring problem *Killing Commendatore*'s narrator can't solve: a literally faceless man who twice asks for a portrait the narrator has no way to paint. On Sasaki's reading, the six-hundred-page novel itself is the substitute portrait, a reading that also recasts the book's final-chapter reveal of its own timeline as a way of approaching the March 11, 2011 disaster without depicting it directly. See Chapter 3.
- **Zaitokukai (在特会)** — A real, documented Japanese ultranationalist organization whose name translates roughly to "citizens against special privileges for [ethnic] Koreans in Japan," centered on anti-Korean-resident rhetoric. Its former leader publicly criticized *Killing Commendatore*'s Nanjing passage. See Chapter 3.
- **The 50-year rule** — The Nobel Prize's own policy of sealing nomination records for fifty years, meaning no public, official confirmation exists of whether a living author (Murakami included) has ever actually been nominated. See Chapter 4.
- **The Haruki Murakami Library** — The public name paired with Waseda University's International House of Literature, holding Murakami's own donated manuscripts and archives and hosting ongoing academic programming. See Chapter 6.
- **Drive My Car** — Ryūsuke Hamaguchi's 2021 film, adapted from three short stories in *Men Without Women* ("Drive My Car," "Scheherazade," and "Kino"), and the first Japanese production ever nominated for the Best Picture Oscar (also nominated for and winning Best International Feature, only the second Japanese film to do so competitively), alongside the first Best Screenplay win for a Japanese writer in Cannes's history. See Chapter 1.

## A NOTE ON THIS SERIES

This is the third and final volume of a three-part critical companion. Volume One, *Murakami, Untranslated*, covered his 1979–1987 debut period and the deliberate, self-taught technique behind his flat, translated-sounding prose. Volume Two, *Murakami, Everywhere*, covered 1988 to 2010, the decade his private alienation became a generation's shared vocabulary. This volume closed out the story from 2013 to the present: the late return to realism, the historical risk of *Killing Commendatore*, the third and final pass at a walled town first imagined in 1980, a Nobel narrative built on considerably less official information than most of its coverage implies, and *Drive My Car*, an adaptation that took one of his shorter stories somewhere Murakami's own prose alone never had.

Taken together, all three volumes have tried to answer one connected question from as many angles as the evidence actually supports: what does it mean that a writer's entire international reputation was built out of a deliberate, decades-long refusal to sound fully at home in his own country, his own language, or his own literary tradition, and what does it say about that reputation that the refusal never really stopped.
