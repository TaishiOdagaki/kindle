Murakami opens 1Q84 by wondering how many people could name a piece of music from its first bars: "very few," he guesses, "almost none." After the book came out, Japan went and bought the record. Fans did not stop at record shopping. They started writing like him, down to the weary sigh yare yare. How did a writer this private become a whole generation's shorthand for loneliness?

Volume One left Murakami in 1987, newly famous and uneasy about it. This volume follows what he did next, from 1988 to 2010: a sequel that arrived six years late, the novel that ended the "too foreign, too light" argument, two catastrophes in ten weeks, a launch that Japan treated like a national event, and a generation of younger writers who learned to write by reading him. You do not need Volume One to follow it.

This book is for you if:

  • You have read The Wind-Up Bird Chronicle, Kafka on the Shore or 1Q84 and want to know what Japan made of them
  • You wonder why his later books feel heavier, and more historical, than the early ones
  • You follow the Nobel Prize every October and want the story behind the reputation
  • You want a critic who takes a position and leaves room for you to disagree

What's inside:

  • The sequel nobody expected, Dance Dance Dance, and the friend the narrator never stopped missing
  • How The Wind-Up Bird Chronicle won over the skeptics, from its spaghetti opening to a soldier fighting for worthless land, and why it earned a Yomiuri Prize
  • 1995 in two parts: the Kobe earthquake and the Tokyo sarin attack, ten weeks apart. Why Murakami spent months listening to survivors for Underground
  • Kafka on the Shore as Japan read it, before the English translation enters the picture
  • 1Q84 as a national event: a 680,000-copy first printing and about 100,000 advance sales in two days
  • The younger novelists nicknamed "Murakami Haruki Children," and Murakami's own words for his stance, "detachment" and "commitment"

Built from primary sources:

First-edition Japanese texts, Murakami's own essays, prize records, and contemporary coverage. Key Japanese passages appear in English, with the original alongside.

About the author

Tyler Atsumori was born and raised in Kyoto to an American mother and a Japanese father, studied modern and contemporary Japanese literature in the Kansai region, and reads the Japanese-language criticism and historical scholarship that English-language writing on Murakami tends to skip. He took up running after reading Murakami's What I Talk About When I Talk About Running and has kept at it for nearly twenty years. Not Murakami's version, which comes with triathlon training. A jog. Every morning he sweats through the same question: what does making sentences have to do with holding your own pace and getting to the end without walking?
