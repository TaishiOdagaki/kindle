## CHAPTER 4: THE NOBEL BET, ONE MORE OCTOBER

**An Annual Ritual**

For about twenty years, every October, part of the Japanese press ran some version of the same story: Murakami is a leading candidate for the Nobel Prize in Literature, the announcement is days away, this could finally be the year. Every October, the Swedish Academy named someone else, and the cycle reset for the following autumn. It became so reliable that "Murakami, snubbed again" turned into a small journalistic genre of its own.

**What the Sealed Records Say**

The coverage tends to skip one detail: the Nobel Prize in Literature does not publish its shortlist. Nominations are sealed for fifty years under the prize's own rules, and surface only long after the fact.[^11] No public, official list confirms that Murakami was ever a finalist in any given year, because the information is not released while a candidate is alive. What drives the annual headlines is far less authoritative: British bookmakers running betting markets on the prize, with the same speculative odds-setting they would apply to a horse race or an election. Murakami's name was a fixture near the top of that market for over a decade, at odds that swung from as short as 3 to 1 to as long as 16 to 1, depending on the bookmaker and the season.[^11] Japanese media treated those odds as a signal from inside the Academy's deliberations. They were a reflection of which names bettors, and the people pricing their bets, recognized.

**Betting Odds Are Not a Signal From Stockholm**

The underlying question, why hadn't he won, still deserves asking, apart from the bookmaker theater built around it. One recurring explanation from Japanese commentators who followed the prize closely points to changes in the Swedish Academy's membership over the years, and the shift in the kind of writer and work that membership tended to recognize. That is a structural, institutional explanation, and a less satisfying one than "he keeps almost winning." It suggests the framing was never about Murakami's work falling just short year after year. A domestic media cycle had built a two-decade "will he finally win" story on top of a betting market that was never reporting anything about the prize.

**What the Bet Was Never About**

The bet did not pay off, and the reason was never that his work fell short. A prize that seals its shortlist for fifty years can only ever be a story other people tell about a writer, and for two decades the story told about Murakami was a story about suspense. It fits the pattern this series has followed from the start. The Akutagawa Prize passed on him twice. The Tanizaki Prize and the Yomiuri Prize came later, from the senior side of the same establishment. The Nobel, so far, has not come at all, and he keeps writing anyway.

[^11]: The Nobel Prize's nomination records are sealed for fifty years under its own rules; as of this writing, only nominations submitted through 1975 have been publicly released. Kōbō Abe, who died in 1993, was widely regarded at the time as one of the Japanese writers closest to winning the prize, though the nomination records confirming any specific consideration remain sealed until decades after this book's publication. On the betting markets themselves: British bookmaker Ladbrokes has listed Murakami among the favorites in most years since at least 2011, at odds that have ranged from roughly 16 to 1 (2011) to 3 to 1 (2013) to 14 to 1 (2024), among other years in between — a spread wide enough on its own to show the odds are tracking public speculation, not any actual signal from the Swedish Academy.
