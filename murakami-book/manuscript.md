# MURAKAMI, UNTRANSLATED

### 1979–1987: The Voice Japan Doubted and the World Fell For

*A Japanese Culture Press Book*

---

In April 1978, a jazz-bar owner named Haruki Murakami watched a batter hit a double at Jingū Stadium and had a thought that surprised him: *I could write a novel.* He'd never written fiction before. This is the story of the voice that decision built, sentence by sentence, and why his own country's most serious literary judges spent years insisting it sounded foreign.

---

## About the Author

Tyler Atsumori was born and raised in Kyoto to an American mother and a Japanese father, and studied modern and contemporary Japanese literature at a university in the Kansai region, where a graduate reading group's slow work through the postwar canon turned into a longer-running project of tracking how Japanese novelists are actually read and argued about at home, as against how they're received once translated abroad. That bilingual upbringing shapes this book directly: it draws on Japanese-language sources most English-language criticism doesn't reach, literary-magazine criticism, prize-jury commentary, and Murakami's own Japanese-language essays, read the way they were actually written, alongside the English-language reception more familiar to this book's readers.

---

## How to Read This Book

A note on who this is for: readers who already love Murakami and want the argument underneath the reputation, and readers who've never read a word of him and just want to know what the fuss is actually about. Either one works. Start here.

Within a year of that afternoon at the ballpark, Murakami had written *Hear the Wind Sing*, and Japan's own literary establishment told him, in print, that his prose read like something in translation. This book is an attempt to find out whether they were right, why, and what that actually meant for the writer he became. It isn't a ranked list of which Murakami novel to read first — plenty of other guides already do that. It's a close look at how one particular voice got built.

Let's get into it.

---

## THE QUESTION THIS WHOLE BOOK IS TRYING TO ANSWER

Here's the question this volume keeps circling back to: is the flat, understated, faintly foreign-sounding "Murakami voice" that English-language readers know something his translators built, or something Murakami built into the Japanese himself, on purpose, as a way of refusing to sound fully Japanese at all? Short answer, worked out over the next nine chapters: he built it. Deliberately. Years before a translator ever touched a page. By the time you reach the verdict chapter, you should have everything you need to argue with that answer yourself. That's the point.

## CHAPTER 1: THE PRIZE HE NEVER WON

**A Career That Wasn't Decorated From the Start**

Zero. That's how many times Japan's most prestigious literary prize ever went to Haruki Murakami, across two separate nominations, at the very start of a career that would go on to sell in the millions of copies, in dozens of languages, with his own name now functioning as something close to a synonym for "contemporary Japanese literature" itself. It's easy to assume a career like that was decorated from the beginning. It wasn't.

**One Prize Won, One Prize Denied Twice**

In 1979, a 29-year-old jazz-bar owner named Haruki Murakami published his first novel, *Hear the Wind Sing* (風の歌を聴け), and won the Gunzō Prize for New Writers: a respected debut award from the literary magazine *Gunzō*, decided that year by unanimous vote of its selection committee, and a legitimate credential.[^1] On the strength of that novel, he was nominated for the 81st Akutagawa Prize, Japan's most prestigious award for new and rising fiction writers, awarded twice yearly and treated, then as now, as close to a career-making stamp of literary legitimacy. He didn't win. The prize that round went to Yoshiko Shigekane's *Yamaai no Kemuri* and Sō Aono's *Gusha no Yoru*.

The following year, his second novel, *Pinball, 1973* (1973年のピンボール), was nominated again. He didn't win that time either, and the jury commentary that survives from that round is considerably less polite than a simple pass-over. Mitsuo Nakamura, one of the panel's judges, wrote that even a skillfully carefree portrait of a young man showing off his fashionable, Westernized affect couldn't actually communicate anything about that young man's interior life, and that trying to capture the era's Americanized manners with an eye that only sees manners, rather than anything underneath them, is a shallow enough vantage point that literature simply can't be born from it — a shame, he added, in a writer who clearly had real talent.[^2] It's a harsher, more specific version of a complaint several jurors on that panel circled that round in different words: that Murakami's prose read as though it had absorbed more from foreign fiction than from his own literary tradition.

**A Complaint Worth Reconsidering**

It's worth sitting with how strange that specific complaint looks in hindsight. The jury wasn't wrong about what they were hearing. They just read it as a flaw instead of a method. As later chapters in this book will get into in some detail, they were reacting to something closer to a genuine technique than they realized: prose that had, in a very literal sense, been passed through English on its way to the page. What Japan's most influential literary prize committee flagged as a young writer's failure to sound sufficiently like himself turned out, decades later, to be one of the more accurate readings anyone gave *Hear the Wind Sing* on its first pass. They just didn't mean it as praise, and Murakami didn't win.

[^1]: *Hear the Wind Sing* won the 22nd Gunzō Prize for New Writers (群像新人文学賞), announced in the June 1979 issue of *Gunzō*, decided unanimously by that year's five-member selection committee. It was submitted under the working title "Happy Birthday, and White Christmas" before *Gunzō*'s editors requested the title be changed.
[^2]: Mitsuo Nakamura, jury remarks (選評) for the 82nd Akutagawa Prize (1980), on *Pinball, 1973*: 「ひとりでハイカラぶってふざけてゐる青年を、彼と同じやうに、いい気で安易な筆づかひで描いても、彼の内面の挙止は一向に伝達されません。現代のアメリカ化した風俗を風俗しか見えぬ浅薄な眼で捕へてゐては、文学は生れ得ない、才能はある人らしいが惜しいことだと思ひます。」

## CHAPTER 2: THE NOVEL WRITTEN BACKWARD

**Writing It Through English First**

He wrote his debut novel's opening chapter twice. Once in English, a language he barely knew. Then he translated his own English back into Japanese. Murakami has told this story several times over the years, most substantially in his own essay collection on his development as a writer.[^3] The broad shape of it, as he's described it himself, goes like this: after starting the novel in Japanese and finding the results flat and unconvincing (too close, in his own account, to the kind of "novelistic" Japanese prose he'd grown up reading and didn't want to reproduce), he tried something else. He wrote a portion of it in English instead, deliberately working within the limited vocabulary and simple sentence structures available to him as a non-native English writer, then translated that English back into Japanese himself. He's described this less as a stunt than as a deliberate search for a plainer, more stripped-down Japanese prose style than the literary Japanese he already knew how to write: a way of discovering a new register in his own language by first filtering his own thinking through a language he had less mastery of, and therefore less inherited baggage in.

**What the Detour Actually Produced**

The effect this produced is more or less exactly what the Akutagawa jury flagged as a weakness a year later: prose with a faint, hard-to-place foreignness to it, even in the original Japanese. Short declarative sentences, an understated emotional register, an avoidance of certain dense literary constructions common in the Japanese fiction of the period. It reads, in the original, a little like something that has already been translated once, because in a real sense it has been.

**Why It Matters Beyond Trivia**

This detail matters for more than trivia value. It means the "Murakami voice" that English-language readers encounter, spare, flat, faintly detached, isn't only a product of what his translators (Alfred Birnbaum first, later Jay Rubin, Philip Gabriel, and Ted Goossen) chose to do with his Japanese. Some of that flatness was already built into the source text, by design, before any of them were involved. A translator rendering Murakami's Japanese into English isn't discovering a foreign text and making it sound natural in English. In a strange, specific way, they're translating something that was partly built by passing through English already — carrying a text back toward a language it had briefly, deliberately, been made to resemble.

**What the Opening Page Actually Does**

It's one thing to describe this technique secondhand. It's another to look at what it actually produces on the page. *Hear the Wind Sing* opens with a flat, aphoristic claim: there's no such thing as perfect writing, the narrator says, the same way there's no such thing as perfect despair, attributed to a writer he says he once knew, left otherwise unnamed. That opening move alone is worth pausing on: a stated universal truth, handed to an anonymous, unspecified source, rather than claimed directly by the narrator himself. It's a device that keeps recurring across the opening page. A throwaway comparison about being able to write something about an elephant but nothing about the man who tends it. A count of years, eight of them, repeated once for emphasis, then closed off with a short, flat verdict, four words in English: a long stretch of time. A sweeping claim about aging and learning, immediately followed by the narrator undercutting his own profundity in the next breath, essentially footnoting himself: that's just a generalization.

That rhythm, a statement, then a short, blunt sentence fragment that either restates it or deflates it, repeats often enough in the space of a single page that it starts to look less like a stylistic tic and more like a structural principle. It's a very different rhythm from the longer, more descriptively elaborated sentences that dominated the Japanese literary fiction Murakami would have grown up reading, and it maps unusually well onto the specific American writers he's named, at different points, as formative: Kurt Vonnegut's habit of deadpan authorial intrusion, undercutting his own sentences almost as soon as he's finished them; Richard Brautigan's fondness for planting an odd, specific, faintly absurd image (an elephant, an elephant keeper) directly inside an otherwise abstract passage; the terse, unsentimental declaratives that define Raymond Chandler's prose, a writer whose *The Long Goodbye* Murakami has called one of the most important books he's ever encountered, and later translated himself. Which specific line owes which specific writer is this book's own reading, not something Murakami has mapped out line by line anywhere on the record. But the three influences themselves are ones he's named directly, and early readers of his debut noticed the American fingerprint immediately, well before anyone had the full story about how the novel was actually assembled.

There's a grammatical detail worth noting too, easy to miss without seeing the actual sentences: whenever other people are doing something in this opening page, the narrator is the object, not the subject. People arrive, talk at him, and pass over him. The text's own image is of them crossing him the way traffic crosses a bridge, with sound and motion, and then gone for good. He becomes the grammatical subject of his own sentence again only once, at the very end of that same passage, and the action he's finally the active agent of is closing his mouth and saying nothing. His one moment of grammatical agency is an act of refusal. That's a sharper detail than simple passivity would be. It's also the template. Critics and readers would spend the next four decades describing nearly every Murakami protagonist the same way: someone things happen to, whose rare moments of actually choosing something are choices to hold back.

[^3]: Murakami Haruki, *Novelist as a Vocation* [職業としての小説家] (Tokyo: Switch Publishing, 2015), Chapter 2, "小説家になった頃" ("The Time I Became a Novelist"). In his own words: he put the English typewriter back in the closet and pulled out manuscript paper and a fountain pen again, then sat at his desk and "translated" (「翻訳」していきました) the roughly one chapter's worth of English he'd written into Japanese — not a rigid, literal translation, he specifies, but something closer to a free "transplantation" (「移植」に近いもの), out of which a new Japanese prose style of his own emerged.

## CHAPTER 3: THE LINE THE JURY WAS ACTUALLY DEFENDING

**Two Categories, One Institution**

There's a distinction that barely exists in English-language book culture but has shaped Japanese literary life for over a century: the line between *junbungaku* (純文学), "pure literature," and *taishū bungaku* (大衆文学), "popular" or "mass" literature. Understand that split and the jury's reaction to *Hear the Wind Sing* stops looking like snobbery and starts looking like something more specific. The division traces back to the Meiji and Taishō eras, when the critic and poet Kitamura Tōkoku helped define *junbungaku* as writing organized around aesthetic and artistic formation rather than entertainment or commercial appeal. In practice, that came to mean fiction judged on the seriousness of its literary ambition, its formal control, and its engagement with the interior life of its characters, as against fiction judged simply on how well it entertained a reader. Japan's two most prominent literary prizes were built directly around this split, and still are: the Akutagawa Prize (芥川賞), the one Murakami was twice nominated for and twice denied, is reserved for *junbungaku*. The Naoki Prize (直木賞), Japan's other major fiction award, is reserved for *taishū bungaku*: genre fiction, entertainment writing, the kind of book built to be read for pleasure rather than studied for craft. An Akutagawa nomination isn't just an award short-list. It's an institutional claim that a given book belongs to the serious side of that divide in the first place.

**What the Jury Actually Said**

This is the frame that makes sense of what actually happened at the 81st Akutagawa Prize jury meeting in 1979 — though it's worth being precise about who actually said what, since the popular version of this story (repeated often enough in English-language commentary that it's worth correcting directly) usually puts words in Kenzaburō Ōe's mouth that the surviving jury record doesn't support. Ōe was on that panel, but the published remarks (選評) for the 81st Prize show no comment from him on *Hear the Wind Sing* at all; his one recorded comment on Murakami comes a year later, on *Pinball, 1973*, and it isn't the dismissal it's often described as (more on that in the next chapter).

What the 81st Prize's jury record actually shows is four jurors weighing in, in strikingly similar terms, on a book they read as visibly shaped by foreign fiction. Saiichi Maruya wrote that Murakami was "trying to show his own individuality while under the influence of American fiction," adding that if this were mere imitation, "the prose wouldn't flow this smoothly, without any stagnation" — a backhanded compliment, crediting the foreign influence while crediting Murakami for doing something with it. Kōsaku Takii found the book's invented world thin in patches, "like the uneven patches in hand-thinned Yoshino paper," but still called Murakami "a writer with a distinctive character" worth watching over the long term. Junnosuke Yoshiyuki doubted the book had quite enough force to survive the Akutagawa's own reputation for putting new writers "through the wringer," while crediting its "ten years of fermentation" as material. And Shūsaku Endō, the sharpest of the four, called it "a novel calculated to an almost hateful degree," one that stripped meaning out of fiction so skillfully he couldn't stop himself asking, in effect, whether that was actually a legitimate thing for a novel to do.[^4] Four different jurors, in four different registers, circling the same basic diagnosis: this reads like something shaped by foreign fiction, executed with real technical control, and nobody on that panel was quite sure yet whether that added up to a serious new voice or a clever trick.

**Not Small-Minded Gatekeeping**

None of this was small-minded gatekeeping for its own sake. Within the *junbungaku* tradition these jurors were operating in, "sounds like a translation" was a genuinely serious charge, because the tradition itself was built, in large part, around cultivating a distinctly Japanese literary voice — one that had spent the better part of a century defining itself partly in relation to, and partly in resistance against, the flood of Western literature that arrived in Japan from the Meiji era onward. A young novelist whose prose read as though it had been filtered through English wasn't just breaking a stylistic convention. He was, in the eyes of jurors steeped in that tradition, declining to do the specific kind of work *junbungaku* existed to reward. What none of them had access to yet, of course, was the actual explanation Murakami would only lay out himself years later, in his own essays: that the "translated" quality they were hearing wasn't an accident of influence. It was assembled, on purpose, sentence by sentence, by a young writer trying to build a new voice by deliberately routing his own Japanese through a language he barely knew.

[^4]: Jury remarks (選評) for the 81st Akutagawa Prize (1979), on *Hear the Wind Sing*: Saiichi Maruya, 「アメリカ小説の影響を受けながら自分の個性を示さうとしてゐます」「もしこれが単なる模倣なら、文章の流れ方がこんなふうに淀みのない調子ではゆかないでせう」; Kōsaku Takii, 「このような架空の作りものは、作品の結晶度が高くなければ駄目だが、これはところどころ薄くて、吉野紙の漉きムラのようなうすく透いてみえるところがあった。しかし、異色のある作家のようで、私は長い眼で見たいと思った」; Junnosuke Yoshiyuki, 「芥川賞というのは新人をもみくちゃにする賞で、それでもかまわないと送り出してもよいだけの力は、この作品にはない。この作品の持味は素材が十年間の醗酵の上に立っているところで」; Shūsaku Endō, 「憎いほど計算した小説である。しかし、この小説は反小説の小説と言うべきであろう」. ## CHAPTER 4: WHAT THE CRITICAL RECORD ACTUALLY SHOWS

**The Tidier Version Isn't What Happened**

It would be tidier, for a book built around a thesis like this one's, if Japan's literary establishment had simply doubted Murakami from the start and never really come around. That's not what happened. It's worth resisting the tidier version twice over here, in fact: not just because the real story is more interesting than a clean arc from doubt to respect, but because one half of that clean arc turns out, on checking the actual record, not to have happened at all.

**Ōe: A Myth Worth Correcting, Not a Reversal**

Kenzaburō Ōe is often cast, in English-language accounts of Murakami's career, as the young writer's first serious establishment critic: the eminent novelist who supposedly took one look at *Hear the Wind Sing* in 1979 and dismissed it as clever imitation going nowhere, only to warm to Murakami's work later, once Murakami's reach became too large to keep dismissing. The published Akutagawa jury record from that round doesn't support the first half of that story. Ōe sat on the 81st Prize's panel, but the surviving remarks show no comment from him on *Hear the Wind Sing* at all — he's recorded with zero lines on that candidate, the same as several other jurors that round. His only documented verdict on either of Murakami's two Akutagawa nominations comes a year later, on *Pinball, 1973*, and it isn't the dismissal it's usually described as.

Noting the book's direct debt to Kurt Vonnegut and its more indirect debt to F. Scott Fitzgerald, Ōe wrote that being able to use what you've taken from other writers as your own working tool, to that degree, "can only be called clear talent" (明らかな才能というほかにないであろう) — a qualified compliment, crediting the imitation as evidence of skill rather than treating it as a dead end.[^5] Ōe went on, fifteen years later, to win the Nobel Prize in Literature himself,[^6] becoming — alongside Yasunari Kawabata a quarter-century earlier — one of only two Japanese laureates in the prize's history at that point. Whatever his private view of Murakami's work did or didn't do after that isn't something this book can pin to a single citable source the way the popular "dismissal to respect" story implies it can. What the documented record actually supports is narrower, and in its own way more interesting: the critic usually remembered as Murakami's harshest early establishment skeptic was, on the one occasion he actually went on record, already crediting him with real, if derivative, talent.

**Kawamoto: From Champion to Skeptic**

Now take an actual trajectory, rather than a corrected non-trajectory: the critic Saburō Kawamoto. Where the Ōe story turned out to be a myth worth retiring, Kawamoto's arc is the real thing — he started as one of Murakami's earliest and most attentive champions and grew more openly critical over time. Kawamoto conducted what's widely cited as the first substantial magazine interview with Murakami, in the literary journal *Cahier* in August 1979, months after *Hear the Wind Sing*'s debut, when Murakami was still a virtual unknown to most of the literary press. Six years later, in 1985, the two collaborated directly, co-authoring a book together, *Eiga wo Meguru Bōken* ("Adventures Concerning Film").[^7] This wasn't a critic keeping a wary, professional distance. This was someone who'd staked an early, public bet on Murakami being worth serious attention, and had the working relationship to show for it.

That relationship soured, at least in print, over what Kawamoto read as a genuine change in Murakami's own project. Reviewing *Underground* (Murakami's 1997 nonfiction book of interviews with survivors of the 1995 Tokyo subway sarin attack, a sharp turn toward direct social engagement after two decades of interior, often surreal fiction), Kawamoto wrote that he couldn't shake a persistent unease throughout the book, wondering why Murakami had suddenly become what Kawamoto called a "shakai-ha" writer, a socially engaged one, and openly questioned whether Murakami himself had simply fallen into telling a familiar, expected kind of story rather than doing anything genuinely new with the material. It's a sharper break than it might look at first: the critic who'd done the first real interview, who'd co-written a book with the man, essentially accusing him of coasting on a predictable narrative shape at exactly the moment Murakami was trying something outside his established mode entirely.

**A Correction and a Real Reversal, Side by Side**

Put the two side by side and the actual picture is more honest, and less symmetrical, than the "critics who changed their minds" framing this chapter opened with implies. Ōe was never the hostile early gatekeeper the popular story makes him out to be; the one thing he's actually on record saying about Murakami's work was already, in its guarded way, a compliment. Kawamoto's reversal, by contrast, is real and well-documented: a critic who staked an early, public bet on Murakami turned around, decades later, and questioned whether the writer he'd once championed was still doing anything genuinely new. What connects a myth worth correcting to a reversal worth taking seriously is the same underlying point: nobody in Japan's literary press, at any point in this story, was treating Murakami the way most of his English-language readers eventually would, as a settled, uncomplicated literary giant. He was, and largely still is, in Japan, an argument that's never quite finished, whether that argument runs the direction popular narratives assume or not.

## CHAPTER 5: THE TRILOGY NOBODY PLANNED

**A Trilogy Named After the Fact**

Three books. One narrator. A friend named only "the Rat" (鼠), who's dead by the time the third book ends. *Hear the Wind Sing* and *Pinball, 1973* share that cast and that first-person narrator, plus a supporting character from the narrator's hometown bar-hopping days, drifting and directionless in a way the narrator himself half-envies and half-worries about. Three years later, *A Wild Sheep Chase* (羊をめぐる冒険) picked the same narrator and the same Rat back up and carried them somewhere considerably stranger. Readers and critics eventually started calling the three books together the "Trilogy of the Rat" (鼠三部作), a name that stuck through fan usage and critical shorthand rather than something Murakami himself coined or announced going in. That distinction matters more than it might look like at first. This wasn't a trilogy in the planned, three-act sense — no outline drafted in advance, no publisher contract for a fixed series. It was a young, still-forming writer returning to the same handful of characters because he apparently wasn't finished with them yet, book by book, without necessarily knowing in 1979 that he'd still be writing about the Rat in 1982.

**Restlessness Between Installments**

The books themselves show real formal restlessness between installments, which cuts against the idea of a tidy, pre-planned arc. *Hear the Wind Sing* runs entirely in the narrator's own first-person voice, looking back on one particular summer. *Pinball, 1973* splits its structure in two: alternating chapters follow the narrator in first person and the Rat in third person, and, this is the detail worth sitting with, the two threads never actually intersect on the page. The narrator and the Rat, still supposedly close friends, spend the entire novel in separate chapters, never sharing a scene. A reading that's become fairly common among critics treats the Rat less as a separate character than as a kind of split-off piece of the narrator's own psyche — his self-destructive, more emotionally exposed half, kept literally structurally apart from the "main" narrating self for an entire book. It's a reading this book finds persuasive without treating it as the only possible one; Murakami hasn't confirmed anything that tidy himself, and one reasonable version of the arc (birth of a double in the first book, separation in the second, a search for that same double ending in his death in the third) is an interpretation of the pattern, not a blueprint Murakami has ever laid out in those terms.

**What Was Actually Happening Off the Page**

What isn't interpretation is what was actually happening in Murakami's own life while he wrote the third book. He and his wife had run a jazz bar, Peter Cat, since his university years (first in Kokubunji, later in Sendagaya) for roughly seven years. He handed off management of the bar in 1981. *A Wild Sheep Chase*, serialized in *Gunzō* in August 1982 and published in book form by Kōdansha that October, was the first novel he wrote as a full-time professional writer rather than someone drafting fiction at the kitchen table after closing up a bar for the night.[^8] It was also, not incidentally, the book that finally won him a major literary prize outright, the 4th Noma Literary New Face Prize (野間文芸新人賞) later that same year,[^8] after two straight Akutagawa nominations had ended in nothing. The trilogy that started as a young bar owner's after-hours hobby closed with its author now doing this for a living, and finally holding a trophy that said, in effect, that Japan's literary establishment was at least willing to take him seriously as a prize-worthy writer, even if the bigger institutional validation of the Akutagawa itself never came.

[^8]: *A Wild Sheep Chase* was serialized in *Gunzō*'s August 1982 issue and published in book form by Kōdansha on October 13, 1982. It won the 4th Noma Literary New Face Prize (野間文芸新人賞) that same year.

**How the Trilogy Actually Ends**

Whatever the Rat's death ends up meaning read back across all three books, on the page it happens almost entirely offstage. The novel's actual final pages aren't about the event itself; they're about the narrator afterward, back in his hometown at J's Bar, handing the bartender money he says he and the Rat earned together and making him a full partner in the place. J, visibly moved, asks for a pinball machine and a jukebox by the time the narrator next comes by; the narrator says he'll have them ready.[^9] Then, after a section break, the actual mourning happens, and it's almost entirely exterior. In the original Japanese: 「僕は川に沿って河口まで歩き、最後に残された五十メートルの砂浜に腰を下ろし、二時間泣いた。そんなに泣いたのは生まれてはじめてだった」 — roughly: he walks along the river to its mouth, sits down on the last fifty meters of beach, and cries for two hours, the first time in his life he's cried that much. He finally stands, brushes the fine sand from his trousers, still not sure where to go next. The novel's very last lines don't dwell on him at all: 「日はすっかり暮れていて、歩き始めると背中に小さな波の音が聞こえた」 — the day gone fully dark, and behind him, as he starts walking, the small sound of waves.[^9] It's a strikingly undemonstrative way to close a trilogy that supposedly ends in a death: no funeral, no confrontation, no explanation delivered on the page, just a bar transaction settled and a controlled, private, time-limited collapse on an anonymous beach — arguably the more honest ending for a character who's spent three books never quite being seen directly by anyone, including, on the trilogy's own account, himself.

## CHAPTER 6: TWO WORLDS, ONE SKULL

**A Split Marked at the Level of Grammar**

One mind. Two voices. Two entirely different pronouns for the same person. *Hard-Boiled Wonderland and the End of the World* (世界の終りとハードボイルド・ワンダーランド), published in 1985, is where the flat, single-register voice of the early trilogy stops being the whole story. The novel runs two narratives in alternating chapters: one following a "Calculator" navigating a paranoid, gadget-dense version of Tokyo, the other following a "Dreamreader" inside a walled, seasonless Town guarded by golden beasts. Both are told in the first person. Both are, by the novel's end, revealed to be the same mind, split into two. And Murakami marks that split at the level of grammar before the plot ever explains it: the Tokyo narrator refers to himself as *watashi* (私), the more formal, distanced first-person pronoun; the Town narrator uses *boku* (僕), the softer, more familiar one that had carried every narrator up to this point. Two pronouns, two registers, one skull.

**What the Openings Sound Like**

The opening pages of each half make the split audible even before you know what it means. The Hard-Boiled Wonderland chapter, titled with a bluntness that borders on comic — 肥満, literally "obesity," which Alfred Birnbaum's English translation renders as "Elevator, Silence, Overweight"[^10] — opens mid-ascent in a stalled, oversized elevator, and the *watashi* narrator immediately loses his grip on the most basic facts available to him. In Birnbaum's translation: *"The elevator continued its impossibly slow ascent. Or at least I imagined it was ascent. There was no telling for sure: it was so slow that all sense of direction simply vanished."* He thinks it was going up, but isn't sure; for all he actually knows it circled the globe and came back. It's a passage built almost entirely out of hedges (*kamoshirenai*/かもしれない, "might be"; *hazu da*/はずだ, "is supposed to be"; *wakaranai*/わからない, "I don't know") stacked one after another, delivered in long, technically precise, almost legalistic sentence structures that read like a man narrating a malfunction report on his own senses. It's the driest kind of "hard-boiled" voice imaginable, applied to something that makes no rational sense at all, and the friction between that dry procedural tone and the absurdity of the situation is the whole comic and unsettling engine of the chapter.

The End of the World chapter, titled just as plainly "Golden Beasts" (金色の獣), could not sound less like it. Where the Tokyo chapter opens on confusion and self-doubt, the Town chapter opens on certainty and rhythm. In Birnbaum's translation: *"With the approach of autumn, a layer of long golden fur grows over their bodies. Golden in the purest sense of the word, with not the least intrusion of another hue."*[^10] The prose moves in long, cyclical, seasonal sweeps: the beasts arriving in spring in their various colors, grazing silently, spring passing into summer, summer into autumn, with none of the *watashi* narrator's hedging vocabulary anywhere in sight. It reads less like a man trying to pin down what's happening around him and more like a fable being recited from memory, patient and unbothered, because in this Town nothing is actually uncertain. It doesn't need to be. That's the whole point of the place, and the prose knows it before a single character explains it.

**One Instinct, Two Registers**

This is a different, more structurally ambitious use of the same basic tool Chapter 2 traced back to *Hear the Wind Sing*: prose style doing thematic work, not just decorating it. The early trilogy used one flattened voice to signal a single kind of guarded detachment. Here, six years later, Murakami builds two entirely distinct registers, one hedging and technical, one mythic and certain, and uses something as small as a pronoun choice to tell the reader, structurally, before the plot catches up, that these two voices belong to the same person wearing two different relationships to his own mind. It's the same instinct toward flatness-as-meaning that got him in trouble with the Akutagawa jury six years earlier, except by now he's found a way to make that instinct do more than one thing at once.

## CHAPTER 7: THE BOOK THAT MADE HIM IMPOSSIBLE TO IGNORE AT HOME

**A Concrete Opening After Two Abstract Ones**

Two years after splitting his narrator's mind in two, Murakami published *Norwegian Wood* (ノルウェイの森) in 1987, and the opening page announces, almost immediately, that whatever he was doing with *Hard-Boiled Wonderland*'s split pronouns and unnamed cities, he isn't doing it here. The novel opens with its narrator, thirty-seven years old, in a seat on a Boeing 747 descending through heavy rain clouds toward Hamburg airport. November rain darkens the ground below; raincoat-clad ground crew, flags mounted on a flat airport building, a BMW billboard. The narrator notes that the whole scene looks like the gloomy background of a Flemish painting. *"Ugh, Germany again,"* he thinks to himself (やれやれ、またドイツか). Once the plane lands, the no-smoking sign switches off and quiet BGM starts playing over the cabin speakers, an orchestral, sweetened cover of the Beatles' "Norwegian Wood." The melody confuses him, he says, the way it always does. Except this time, he corrects himself immediately, it hits him far harder and more violently than it ever has before.

Set this next to the two previous chapters and the departure is immediate and total. No walled Town, no unnamed elevator, no narrator uncertain which direction he's even moving in. Hamburg is a real city. A Boeing 747 is a real, specific aircraft. BMW is a real brand, visible on a real billboard. This is the most geographically and materially concrete opening Murakami had written up to this point in his career, and it arrives after two straight books built almost entirely out of deliberate placelessness. The pronoun is back to a single, familiar *boku*. No structural split this time, because there's only one mind in this novel, worrying over one specific, painful memory, not two halves of a psyche negotiating with each other across alternating chapters.

**The Trigger Mechanism**

The trigger mechanism matters too. Nothing about the opening page is abstract or aphoristic the way *Hear the Wind Sing*'s was; instead, it's a very conventional, almost old-fashioned device: a piece of music on an airplane sound system, triggering an overwhelming wave of memory the narrator is about to spend the rest of the novel unpacking. It's worth noting, too, that "*yare yare*" (やれやれ), that small, resigned sigh of a phrase, "ugh" or "well, well," shows up here as Watanabe registers the German billboard below him — though the tic itself wasn't new by 1987. It first turns up in *Pinball, 1973* (1980), seven years earlier, and is already a fully established habit of voice by *A Wild Sheep Chase* (1982), five years earlier.[^13] By the time it opens *Norwegian Wood*, it's a well-worn tool from Murakami's own kit, not a new one, deployed here to color the most concrete, least surreal opening he'd written up to that point.

**Same Architecture, Opposite Function**

And the self-correcting rhythm from *Hear the Wind Sing*'s opening page, state something, then immediately qualify or intensify it, is still here, technically, if in a gentler, more compressed form. In Jay Rubin's English translation: *"The melody never failed to send a shudder through me, but this time it hit me harder than ever."*[^14] The sentence still runs on the same basic hinge Chapter 2 traced back to his debut, a baseline claim, then a correction that pushes past it, just folded into a single sentence instead of built across two, and without the flatter, more clinical tone of the earlier novels' corrections. What's changed is the direction that correction runs. In *Hear the Wind Sing*, that rhythm almost always deflated: a big claim, punctured immediately by a flat, dry qualifier. Here, at thirty-seven, on a descending plane, it intensifies instead. The correction pushes toward more feeling, not less. Same architecture, opposite function. A writer who'd built his entire early reputation on undercutting his own sentences had, by 1987, found a way to use the identical mechanism to let one hit harder.

That shift in mode is exactly what made *Norwegian Wood* land so differently at home than anything he'd written before it.

## CHAPTER 8: THE BACKLASH TO THE BESTSELLER

**A Jump Nothing Had Prepared Him For**

*Norwegian Wood* sold at a scale nothing in Murakami's career had prepared him for, and nothing in Japanese publishing at the time had quite prepared the industry for either: cumulative sales in Japan alone that would eventually pass ten million copies,[^11] against a career built, up to that point, on novels Murakami himself has described as selling around a hundred thousand copies apiece. Murakami has since said, plainly, that the experience of that jump left him feeling profoundly isolated, that success on that scale made him feel, in his own words, disliked rather than embraced, even as the book turned him into a genuine mainstream phenomenon rather than a writer with a devoted but contained readership.

**The Tagline He Wrote Himself**

Some of that discomfort traces back to a decision Murakami made himself, before a single copy sold: the marketing line "100% a love novel" (100%の恋愛小説) used to promote the book on its release. He wrote that line himself. And he's since said, in his own words, that what he'd actually wanted to signal wasn't "love novel" at all, but something closer to "100% a realist novel" (a way of marking, plainly, that this book was a deliberate departure from the surreal registers of *A Wild Sheep Chase* and *Hard-Boiled Wonderland and the End of the World*, or as he's put it himself, "different lines" from those books), and that he reached for "love novel," a retro, slightly tongue-in-cheek, deliberately outdated phrase, as a piece of wordplay instead, since the more literal label wasn't something he felt he could put on the book's own obi band.[^12] It stuck in a way the more accurate label never got the chance to. Readers and critics who took the tagline at face value found a novel more preoccupied with grief, memory, and the specific damage of a friend's suicide than with anything resembling a conventional romance. The actual relationships in the book are unstable, painful, arbitrary, closer to case studies in loss than to a love story built for easy consumption. The book that made him a household name in Japan is also, on a fair reading of what's actually on the page, one of the more miscategorized bestsellers of his career, and largely because of a label he chose for himself.

That gap between the marketing and the book underneath it fed directly into the tension Chapter 3 already laid out. A novel this commercially enormous, sold under a tagline this reductive, was always going to read to a skeptical literary establishment as further evidence that Murakami belonged more to *taishū bungaku*, mass entertainment, than to the *junbungaku* tradition the Akutagawa jury had already found him wanting against in 1979 and 1980. Where the earlier trilogy and *Hard-Boiled Wonderland* had at least been strange enough, formally, to argue over on literary terms, *Norwegian Wood* was the book that made Murakami a figure the whole country had an opinion about — which is exactly the kind of visibility that tends to work against a writer's standing with an establishment that has historically treated broad popularity itself as faintly suspicious.

**Leaving Rather Than Fighting the Label**

Murakami's own response to all of this wasn't to stay and fight the label. He and his wife had already left Japan in October 1986, nearly a year before *Norwegian Wood* was published, settling for a period on Spetses and then Mykonos in Greece before moving on to Italy: a stretch of time he's described as an unusually nomadic period even by his own standards, one spent translating other writers' work and drafting *Norwegian Wood* itself far from Tokyo. He remained abroad through the book's publication and the sales explosion that followed it, a trip that had started as ordinary expatriate restlessness stretching into something that, by his own later account of how the book's success actually felt to him, reads like distance kept on purpose, from a level of fame that had stopped feeling like a reward and started feeling like exposure. The most commercially successful book of his career to that point was also the one that sent him furthest from home. A decade of being told he sounded too foreign, and the moment he finally became impossible to ignore, he wasn't even in the country.

## CHAPTER 9: VERDICT — WHAT UNTRANSLATED MEANS BY THE END OF ACT ONE

**What the Technique Actually Was**

This book opened with a question: is the flat, understated, faintly foreign-sounding voice English-language readers know as "Murakami" something his translators built, or something he built himself, in Japanese, before any of them arrived? By the end of this first act of his career, 1979 to 1987, debut to *Norwegian Wood*, the answer is about as clear as literary history gets. He built it. Deliberately, by his own later account, sentence by sentence, by routing his own first novel through English and back. The jury that read the result in 1979 heard exactly what was there, prose with a foreign fingerprint on it, and simply filed that observation under the wrong heading, as a failure of craft rather than a description of method.

Everything that happened across the next eight years complicates that origin story without overturning it. *Hear the Wind Sing* and its two sequels carried the flat voice forward as a single, consistent register, tied to a Rat who may or may not have been a separated piece of the narrator's own psyche. *Hard-Boiled Wonderland* split that single voice into two, using a pronoun choice most readers would never consciously register, *watashi* against *boku*, to mark a fracture in one mind across two entirely different registers of prose. *Norwegian Wood* then did something that looks, on the surface, like an about-face: real cities, a real airline, a narrator locked into a single unadorned first person, no structural trickery at all. But even there, the same underlying mechanism Chapter 2 first identified, a claim, followed immediately by a correcting beat, was still doing the work, just pointed in the opposite direction, toward more feeling rather than less. The technique never left. Only what it was being used for kept changing.

**What Never Resolved at Home**

What also never resolved, across this same stretch, was Japan's own verdict on what to make of any of it. Two Akutagawa nominations came back empty, on the specific grounds that the prose sounded too imported. A juror who dismissed him in 1979 came to respect him by the 1990s; a critic who championed him from the very first magazine interview in 1979 turned skeptical of him by 1997. And the book that finally made him inescapable at home (ten million copies, a tagline he wrote and later disowned, a whole country with an opinion about him) sent him running for Greece and Italy rather than settling the argument in his favor. If anything, it sharpened the oldest version of the complaint: too big to be taken seriously as *junbungaku*, too strange in its earlier surreal registers to be comfortable *taishū bungaku* either. Murakami spent this entire first act of his career being read, at home, as something that didn't quite fit the available categories.

**The Throughline**

That, it turns out, is the actual throughline connecting the prose technique to the reception history, and it's a tighter connection than it might look like on first pass. A writer who built his own voice out of a deliberate refusal to sound fully at home in his own language spent the same years being read, by his own country's critical establishment, as someone who didn't quite belong to its literary tradition either. The not-quite-belonging wasn't only a stylistic accident that happened to draw institutional suspicion. It was the same condition, showing up twice — once in the sentences themselves, once in how those sentences were received. English-language readers who fell for the flat, faintly displaced voice of the *translated* Murakami were responding, without knowing it, to a writer whose relationship to his own native language had never been entirely settled in the first place. That's not a story translation can take full credit for. It's a story translation simply carried forward, into languages where "not quite sounding native" reads less like a flaw and more like an entire literary identity.

The Murakami who leaves Greece and Italy in the years after *Norwegian Wood* is not yet finished being argued over — not by Japan's critics, and not by his own ambitions for what his fiction could do next. That argument, and the far larger books that came out of it, is where the next volume picks up.

## GLOSSARY

- **Junbungaku (純文学)** — "Pure literature": the Japanese literary tradition organized around artistic and aesthetic ambition, judged on formal control and craft rather than commercial appeal. The Akutagawa Prize is reserved for work in this category. See Chapter 3.
- **Taishū bungaku (大衆文学)** — "Mass" or "popular" literature: commercial, entertainment-oriented fiction, the category the Naoki Prize (Japan's other major fiction award) recognizes. See Chapter 3.
- **The Akutagawa Prize (芥川賞)** — Japan's most prestigious award for new and rising *junbungaku* writers, awarded twice yearly. Murakami was nominated twice (1979, 1980) and won neither time. See Chapters 1 and 3.
- **The Gunzō Prize for New Writers (群像新人文学賞)** — The debut-writer's award from the literary magazine *Gunzō* that *Hear the Wind Sing* won in 1979, distinct from and preceding its Akutagawa nomination. See Chapter 1.
- **The Trilogy of the Rat (鼠三部作)** — The fan- and critic-applied name (not Murakami's own designation) for *Hear the Wind Sing*, *Pinball, 1973*, and *A Wild Sheep Chase*, connected by a shared narrator and his friend "the Rat." See Chapter 5.
- **Watashi (私) / Boku (僕)** — Two of several Japanese first-person pronouns, differing in formality and register. *Hard-Boiled Wonderland and the End of the World* uses each for a different half of its split narrative. See Chapter 6.
