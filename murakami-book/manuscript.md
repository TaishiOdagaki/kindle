# MURAKAMI, UNTRANSLATED

### The Voice Japan Doubted and the World Fell For, From a Japanese Researcher to Readers Who Only Know Him in English

*A Japanese Culture Press Book*

## A Note Before You Begin

This book is an independent work of literary criticism and research about the writer Haruki Murakami and his work. It is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or representing Murakami himself, his publishers (Kodansha, Shinchosha, and others in Japan; Knopf and Vintage in English translation), or any of his translators. No extended passages from Murakami's novels or essays are reproduced here; quotations are kept brief and used for critical commentary, consistent with fair-use practice for literary criticism.

Where a claim about a living person's stated intentions, private life, or ongoing career could plausibly have changed or been characterized differently elsewhere, that is noted directly in the text rather than smoothed into false certainty.

## About the Author

Jiro Naozane is an independent researcher and writer covering Japanese culture (anime, manga, food culture, and now literature) for over a decade, after studying Japanese culture at university, where a student research circle turned casual curiosity into something closer to an ongoing project. This book draws directly on Japanese-language sources: literary-magazine criticism, prize-jury commentary, and Murakami's own Japanese-language essays that have shaped how he's actually been read and argued about at home, alongside the English-language reception more familiar to this book's readers.

## How to Read This Book

This isn't a plot-summary guide to Murakami's novels, and it isn't a ranked list of which book to read first. Plenty of other resources already do that. This is an attempt to answer a specific, underexamined question: why a writer treated with real suspicion by Japan's own literary establishment for most of his early career became the most widely read Japanese novelist alive anywhere else in the world, and whether the voice international readers love was, in a strange and literal sense, built for translation before a single translator ever touched it.

## THE QUESTION THIS WHOLE BOOK IS TRYING TO ANSWER

Is the "Murakami voice" that English-language readers know (flat, understated, faintly foreign-sounding even in translation) something translators created, or something Murakami built into the original Japanese himself, deliberately, as an act of not quite wanting to sound Japanese at all?

## CHAPTER 1: THE PRIZE HE NEVER WON

Haruki Murakami is, by any ordinary measure, one of the most successful novelists alive. His books sell in the millions across dozens of languages, he's been a perennial name in Nobel Prize speculation for over a decade, and outside Japan he functions as something close to a synonym for "contemporary Japanese literature" itself, whether that reputation is fair to the rest of the field or not. It's easy to assume a career like that must have been decorated from the very beginning. It wasn't.

In 1979, a 29-year-old jazz-bar owner named Haruki Murakami published his first novel, *Hear the Wind Sing* (風の歌を聴け), and won the Gunzō Prize for New Writers: a respected debut award from the literary magazine *Gunzō*, and a legitimate credential. On the strength of that novel, he was nominated for the 81st Akutagawa Prize, Japan's most prestigious award for new and rising fiction writers, awarded twice yearly and treated, then as now, as close to a career-making stamp of literary legitimacy. He didn't win. The prize that round went to Yoshiko Shigekane's *Yamaai no Kemuri* and Sō Aono's *Gusha no Yoru*. The following year, his second novel, *Pinball, 1973* (1973年のピンボール), was nominated again. He didn't win that time either, and the jury commentary that survives from that round is considerably less polite than a simple pass-over. One recurring criticism, repeated in various forms across the panel's remarks, was that the prose read as though its author had spent too much time reading foreign novels in translation: 外国翻訳小説の読み過ぎ, roughly "too much reading of foreign translated novels," offered as a criticism, not a compliment.

It's worth sitting with how strange that specific complaint looks in hindsight. The jury wasn't wrong about what they were hearing. They just read it as a flaw instead of a method. As later chapters in this book will get into in some detail, they were reacting to something closer to a genuine technique than they realized: prose that had, in a very literal sense, been passed through English on its way to the page. What Japan's most influential literary prize committee flagged as a young writer's failure to sound sufficiently like himself turned out, decades later, to be one of the more accurate readings anyone gave *Hear the Wind Sing* on its first pass. They just didn't mean it as praise, and Murakami didn't win.

## CHAPTER 2: THE NOVEL WRITTEN BACKWARD

Murakami has told the story of how he actually wrote *Hear the Wind Sing* several times over the years, most substantially in his own essay collection on his development as a writer. The broad shape of the story, as he's described it himself, goes like this: after starting the novel in Japanese and finding the results flat and unconvincing (too close, in his own account, to the kind of "novelistic" Japanese prose he'd grown up reading and didn't want to reproduce), he tried something else. He wrote a portion of it in English instead, deliberately working within the limited vocabulary and simple sentence structures available to him as a non-native English writer, then translated that English back into Japanese himself. He's described this less as a stunt than as a deliberate search for a plainer, more stripped-down Japanese prose style than the literary Japanese he already knew how to write: a way of discovering a new register in his own language by first filtering his own thinking through a language he had less mastery of, and therefore less inherited baggage in.

The effect this produced is more or less exactly what the Akutagawa jury flagged as a weakness a year later: prose with a faint, hard-to-place foreignness to it, even in the original Japanese. Short declarative sentences, an understated emotional register, an avoidance of certain dense literary constructions common in the Japanese fiction of the period. It reads, in the original, a little like something that has already been translated once, because in a real sense it has been.

This detail matters for more than trivia value. It means the "Murakami voice" that English-language readers encounter, spare, flat, faintly detached, isn't only a product of what his translators (Alfred Birnbaum first, later Jay Rubin, Philip Gabriel, and Ted Goossen) chose to do with his Japanese. Some of that flatness was already built into the source text, by design, before any of them were involved. A translator rendering Murakami's Japanese into English isn't discovering a foreign text and making it sound natural in English. In a strange, specific way, they're translating something that was partly built by passing through English already — carrying a text back toward a language it had briefly, deliberately, been made to resemble.

**What the Opening Page Actually Does**

It's one thing to describe this technique secondhand. It's another to look at what it actually produces on the page. *Hear the Wind Sing* opens with a flat, aphoristic claim: there's no such thing as perfect writing, the narrator says, the same way there's no such thing as perfect despair, attributed to a writer he says he once knew, left otherwise unnamed. That opening move alone is worth pausing on: a stated universal truth, handed to an anonymous, unspecified source, rather than claimed directly by the narrator himself. It's a device that keeps recurring across the opening page. A throwaway comparison about being able to write something about an elephant but nothing about the man who tends it. A count of years, eight of them, repeated once for emphasis, then closed off with a short, flat verdict, four words in English: a long stretch of time. A sweeping claim about aging and learning, immediately followed by the narrator undercutting his own profundity in the next breath, essentially footnoting himself: that's just a generalization.

That rhythm, a statement, then a short, blunt sentence fragment that either restates it or deflates it, repeats often enough in the space of a single page that it starts to look less like a stylistic tic and more like a structural principle. It's a very different rhythm from the longer, more descriptively elaborated sentences that dominated the Japanese literary fiction Murakami would have grown up reading, and it maps unusually well onto the specific American writers he's named, at different points, as formative: Kurt Vonnegut's habit of deadpan authorial intrusion, undercutting his own sentences almost as soon as he's finished them; Richard Brautigan's fondness for planting an odd, specific, faintly absurd image (an elephant, an elephant keeper) directly inside an otherwise abstract passage; the terse, unsentimental declaratives that define Raymond Chandler's prose, a writer whose *The Long Goodbye* Murakami has called one of the most important books he's ever encountered, and later translated himself. Which specific line owes which specific writer is this book's own reading, not something Murakami has mapped out line by line anywhere on the record. But the three influences themselves are ones he's named directly, and early readers of his debut noticed the American fingerprint immediately, well before anyone had the full story about how the novel was actually assembled.

There's a grammatical detail worth noting too, easy to miss without seeing the actual sentences: whenever other people are doing something in this opening page, the narrator is the object, not the subject. People arrive, talk at him, and pass over him. The text's own image is of them crossing him the way traffic crosses a bridge, with sound and motion, and then gone for good. He becomes the grammatical subject of his own sentence again only once, at the very end of that same passage, and the action he's finally the active agent of is closing his mouth and saying nothing. His one moment of grammatical agency is an act of refusal. That's a sharper detail than simple passivity would be, and it's the same narrow, withholding kind of agency critics and readers would spend the next four decades describing as the defining emotional register of nearly every Murakami protagonist who came after him: someone who mostly has things happen to him, and whose rare moments of choosing anything are choices to hold back.

## CHAPTER 3: WHAT "LIGHT" MEANT AS AN INSULT

To understand why a panel of respected literary judges reacted to *Hear the Wind Sing* the way they did, it helps to understand a distinction that barely exists in English-language book culture but has shaped Japanese literary life for over a century: the line between *junbungaku* (純文学), "pure literature," and *taishū bungaku* (大衆文学), "popular" or "mass" literature. The division traces back to the Meiji and Taishō eras, when the critic and poet Kitamura Tōkoku helped define *junbungaku* as writing organized around aesthetic and artistic formation rather than entertainment or commercial appeal. In practice, that came to mean fiction judged on the seriousness of its literary ambition, its formal control, and its engagement with the interior life of its characters, as against fiction judged simply on how well it entertained a reader. Japan's two most prominent literary prizes were built directly around this split, and still are: the Akutagawa Prize (芥川賞), the one Murakami was twice nominated for and twice denied, is reserved for *junbungaku*. The Naoki Prize (直木賞), Japan's other major fiction award, is reserved for *taishū bungaku*: genre fiction, entertainment writing, the kind of book built to be read for pleasure rather than studied for craft. An Akutagawa nomination isn't just an award short-list. It's an institutional claim that a given book belongs to the serious side of that divide in the first place.

This is the frame that makes sense of what actually happened at the 81st Akutagawa Prize jury meeting in 1979. Kenzaburō Ōe, at that point already one of Japan's most respected literary novelists, and, fifteen years later, a Nobel laureate himself, reviewed *Hear the Wind Sing* and judged that it skillfully imitated contemporary American fiction without pushing its author toward developing anything of his own, calling the result, in effect, a dead end for both writer and reader rather than a real step forward. Another juror on the same panel, the novelist and critic Saiichi Maruya, made a related but distinct observation: that the book read as heavily shaped by contemporary American fiction, with a certain recognizably Japanese emotional sensibility layered on top of what was, underneath, an American-style novel. Different jurors, converging on the same basic diagnosis: this reads like something translated, or something written by someone who'd been reading in English more than he'd been reading in Japanese. A year later, *Pinball, 1973* drew the same complaint even more bluntly — that its author had clearly been reading too much foreign fiction in translation.

None of this was small-minded gatekeeping for its own sake. Within the *junbungaku* tradition these jurors were operating in, "sounds like a translation" was a genuinely serious charge, because the tradition itself was built, in large part, around cultivating a distinctly Japanese literary voice — one that had spent the better part of a century defining itself partly in relation to, and partly in resistance against, the flood of Western literature that arrived in Japan from the Meiji era onward. A young novelist whose prose read as though it had been filtered through English wasn't just breaking a stylistic convention. He was, in the eyes of jurors steeped in that tradition, declining to do the specific kind of work *junbungaku* existed to reward. What none of them had access to yet, of course, was the actual explanation Murakami would only lay out himself years later, in his own essays: that the "translated" quality they were hearing wasn't an accident of influence. It was assembled, on purpose, sentence by sentence, by a young writer trying to build a new voice by deliberately routing his own Japanese through a language he barely knew.

## CHAPTER 4: THE CRITICS WHO CHANGED THEIR MINDS — IN BOTH DIRECTIONS

It would be tidier, for a book built around a thesis like this one's, if Japan's literary establishment had simply doubted Murakami from the start and never really come around. That's not what happened, and it's worth resisting the tidier version, because the real story is more interesting and considerably less flattering to any single narrative — including this book's own.

Take Kenzaburō Ōe, the same juror whose 1979 Akutagawa comments dismissed *Hear the Wind Sing* as skillful imitation leading nowhere. Ōe went on, fifteen years later, to win the Nobel Prize in Literature himself, becoming — alongside Yasunari Kawabata a quarter-century earlier — one of only two Japanese laureates in the prize's history at that point. In the years that followed his own Nobel win, Ōe's public relationship to Murakami's work softened considerably; by later in his career, he was willing to credit Murakami's fiction with reaching and holding a genuinely wide readership, treating that reach as something worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as evidence of shallowness. The two men, by most accounts, developed a measure of mutual professional respect that would have been hard to predict from the 81st Akutagawa Prize jury notes alone. It's worth noting that Murakami had reportedly been reading Ōe's own work since high school and considered him one of the defining Japanese novelists of the postwar period, well before either man's relationship to the other's work became public literary history.

Now take the opposite trajectory: the critic Saburō Kawamoto. Where Ōe started skeptical and warmed, Kawamoto started as one of Murakami's earliest and most attentive champions and grew more openly critical over time. Kawamoto conducted what's widely cited as the first substantial magazine interview with Murakami, in the literary journal *Cahier* in August 1979, months after *Hear the Wind Sing*'s debut, when Murakami was still a virtual unknown to most of the literary press. Six years later, in 1985, the two collaborated directly, co-authoring a book together, *Eiga wo Meguru Bōken* ("Adventures Concerning Film"). This wasn't a critic keeping a wary, professional distance. This was someone who'd staked an early, public bet on Murakami being worth serious attention, and had the working relationship to show for it.

That relationship soured, at least in print, over what Kawamoto read as a genuine change in Murakami's own project. Reviewing *Underground* (Murakami's 1997 nonfiction book of interviews with survivors of the 1995 Tokyo subway sarin attack, a sharp turn toward direct social engagement after two decades of interior, often surreal fiction), Kawamoto wrote that he couldn't shake a persistent unease throughout the book, wondering why Murakami had suddenly become what Kawamoto called a "shakai-ha" writer, a socially engaged one, and openly questioned whether Murakami himself had simply fallen into telling a familiar, expected kind of story rather than doing anything genuinely new with the material. It's a sharper break than it might look at first: the critic who'd done the first real interview, who'd co-written a book with the man, essentially accusing him of coasting on a predictable narrative shape at exactly the moment Murakami was trying something outside his established mode entirely.

Put the two trajectories side by side and the actual picture is more honest than either extreme: Japan's critical relationship to Murakami was never a single, static verdict, in either direction. It moved. A juror who once dismissed him as derivative ended up respecting the size and durability of his readership. A critic who once championed him early ended up publicly doubting his motives at the exact moment Murakami tried to prove he was more than a genre writer working the same groove. What stayed constant, on both sides of that shifting critical weather, was that nobody in Japan's literary press was treating Murakami the way most of his English-language readers eventually would: as a settled, uncomplicated literary giant. He was, and largely still is, in Japan, an argument that's never quite finished.

## CHAPTER 5: THE TRILOGY NOBODY PLANNED

*Hear the Wind Sing* and *Pinball, 1973* share a cast, a first-person narrator, and a supporting character named only "the Rat" (鼠), a friend of the narrator's from his hometown bar-hopping days, drifting and directionless in a way the narrator himself half-envies and half-worries about. Three years later, *A Wild Sheep Chase* (羊をめぐる冒険) picked the same narrator and the same Rat back up and carried them somewhere considerably stranger. Readers and critics eventually started calling the three books together the "Trilogy of the Rat" (鼠三部作), a name that stuck through fan usage and critical shorthand rather than something Murakami himself coined or announced going in. That distinction matters more than it might look like at first. This wasn't a trilogy in the planned, three-act sense — no outline drafted in advance, no publisher contract for a fixed series. It was a young, still-forming writer returning to the same handful of characters because he apparently wasn't finished with them yet, book by book, without necessarily knowing in 1979 that he'd still be writing about the Rat in 1982.

The books themselves show real formal restlessness between installments, which cuts against the idea of a tidy, pre-planned arc. *Hear the Wind Sing* runs entirely in the narrator's own first-person voice, looking back on one particular summer. *Pinball, 1973* splits its structure in two: alternating chapters follow the narrator in first person and the Rat in third person, and, this is the detail worth sitting with, the two threads never actually intersect on the page. The narrator and the Rat, still supposedly close friends, spend the entire novel in separate chapters, never sharing a scene. A reading that's become fairly common among critics treats the Rat less as a separate character than as a kind of split-off piece of the narrator's own psyche — his self-destructive, more emotionally exposed half, kept literally structurally apart from the "main" narrating self for an entire book. It's a reading this book finds persuasive without treating it as the only possible one; Murakami hasn't confirmed anything that tidy himself, and one reasonable version of the arc (birth of a double in the first book, separation in the second, a search for that same double ending in his death in the third) is an interpretation of the pattern, not a blueprint Murakami has ever laid out in those terms.

What isn't interpretation is what was actually happening in Murakami's own life while he wrote the third book. He and his wife had run a jazz bar, Peter Cat, since his university years (first in Kokubunji, later in Sendagaya) for roughly seven years. He handed off management of the bar in 1981. *A Wild Sheep Chase*, published the following year, was the first novel he wrote as a full-time professional writer rather than someone drafting fiction at the kitchen table after closing up a bar for the night. It was also, not incidentally, the book that finally won him a major literary prize outright, the Noma Literary New Face Prize in 1982, after two straight Akutagawa nominations had ended in nothing. The trilogy that started as a young bar owner's after-hours hobby closed with its author now doing this for a living, and finally holding a trophy that said, in effect, that Japan's literary establishment was at least willing to take him seriously as a prize-worthy writer, even if the bigger institutional validation of the Akutagawa itself never came.

## CHAPTER 6: TWO WORLDS, ONE SKULL

*Hard-Boiled Wonderland and the End of the World* (世界の終りとハードボイルド・ワンダーランド), published in 1985, is where the flat, single-register voice of the early trilogy stops being the whole story. The novel runs two narratives in alternating chapters: one following a "Calculator" navigating a paranoid, gadget-dense version of Tokyo, the other following a "Dreamreader" inside a walled, seasonless Town guarded by golden beasts. Both are told in the first person. Both are, by the novel's end, revealed to be the same mind, split into two. And Murakami marks that split at the level of grammar before the plot ever explains it: the Tokyo narrator refers to himself as *watashi* (私), the more formal, distanced first-person pronoun; the Town narrator uses *boku* (僕), the softer, more familiar one that had carried every narrator up to this point. Two pronouns, two registers, one skull.

The opening pages of each half make the split audible even before you know what it means. The Hard-Boiled Wonderland chapter, titled with a bluntness that borders on comic, "Elevator, Silence, Obesity" (エレベーター、無音、肥満), opens mid-ascent in a stalled, oversized elevator, and the *watashi* narrator immediately loses his grip on the most basic facts available to him: *"The elevator continued its ascent at an extremely slow speed"* (エレベーターはきわめて緩慢な速度で上昇をつづけていた), he says, and then spends the next several long, syntactically overloaded sentences walking that certainty back. He thinks it was going up, but isn't sure; *"the sense of direction itself had vanished"* (方向の感覚というものが消滅してしまっていた); for all he actually knows it circled the globe and came back. It's a passage built almost entirely out of hedges (*kamoshirenai*/かもしれない, "might be"; *hazu da*/はずだ, "is supposed to be"; *wakaranai*/わからない, "I don't know") stacked one after another, delivered in long, technically precise, almost legalistic sentence structures that read like a man narrating a malfunction report on his own senses. It's the driest kind of "hard-boiled" voice imaginable, applied to something that makes no rational sense at all, and the friction between that dry procedural tone and the absurdity of the situation is the whole comic and unsettling engine of the chapter.

The End of the World chapter, titled just as plainly "Golden Beasts" (金色の獣), could not sound less like it. Where the Tokyo chapter opens on confusion and self-doubt, the Town chapter opens on certainty and rhythm: *"When autumn came, their bodies became covered in long golden fur. It was gold in the purest sense"* (秋がやってくると、彼らの体は毛足の長い金色の体毛に覆われることになった。それは純粋な意味での金色だった). The prose moves in long, cyclical, seasonal sweeps: the beasts arriving in spring in their various colors, grazing silently, spring passing into summer, summer into autumn, with none of the *watashi* narrator's hedging vocabulary anywhere in sight. It reads less like a man trying to pin down what's happening around him and more like a fable being recited from memory, patient and unbothered, because in this Town nothing is actually uncertain. It doesn't need to be. That's the whole point of the place, and the prose knows it before a single character explains it.

This is a different, more structurally ambitious use of the same basic tool Chapter 2 traced back to *Hear the Wind Sing*: prose style doing thematic work, not just decorating it. The early trilogy used one flattened voice to signal a single kind of guarded detachment. Here, six years later, Murakami builds two entirely distinct registers, one hedging and technical, one mythic and certain, and uses something as small as a pronoun choice to tell the reader, structurally, before the plot catches up, that these two voices belong to the same person wearing two different relationships to his own mind. It's the same instinct toward flatness-as-meaning that got him in trouble with the Akutagawa jury six years earlier, except by now he's found a way to make that instinct do more than one thing at once.

## CHAPTER 7: THE BOOK THAT MADE HIM IMPOSSIBLE TO IGNORE AT HOME

Two years after splitting his narrator's mind in two, Murakami published *Norwegian Wood* (ノルウェイの森) in 1987, and the opening page announces, almost immediately, that whatever he was doing with *Hard-Boiled Wonderland*'s split pronouns and unnamed cities, he isn't doing it here. The novel opens with its narrator, thirty-seven years old, in a seat on a Boeing 747 descending through heavy rain clouds toward Hamburg airport. November rain darkens the ground below; raincoat-clad ground crew, flags mounted on a flat airport building, a BMW billboard. The narrator notes that the whole scene looks like the gloomy background of a Flemish painting. *"Ugh, Germany again,"* he thinks to himself (やれやれ、またドイツか). Once the plane lands, the no-smoking sign switches off and quiet BGM starts playing over the cabin speakers, an orchestral, sweetened cover of the Beatles' "Norwegian Wood." The melody confuses him, he says, the way it always does. Except this time, he corrects himself immediately, it hits him far harder and more violently than it ever has before.

Set this next to the two previous chapters and the departure is immediate and total. No walled Town, no unnamed elevator, no narrator uncertain which direction he's even moving in. Hamburg is a real city. A Boeing 747 is a real, specific aircraft. BMW is a real brand, visible on a real billboard. This is the most geographically and materially concrete opening Murakami had written up to this point in his career, and it arrives after two straight books built almost entirely out of deliberate placelessness. The pronoun is back to a single, familiar *boku*. No structural split this time, because there's only one mind in this novel, worrying over one specific, painful memory, not two halves of a psyche negotiating with each other across alternating chapters.

The trigger mechanism matters too. Nothing about the opening page is abstract or aphoristic the way *Hear the Wind Sing*'s was; instead, it's a very conventional, almost old-fashioned device: a piece of music on an airplane sound system, triggering an overwhelming wave of memory the narrator is about to spend the rest of the novel unpacking. It's worth noting, too, that "*yare yare*" (やれやれ), that small, resigned sigh of a phrase, "ugh" or "well, well," shows up here early and would go on to become one of the most recognizable verbal tics in Murakami's entire body of work, repeated by narrators across nearly every novel that followed. This may be one of its earliest appearances in his fiction, a small habit of voice that started here and never left.

And the self-correcting rhythm from *Hear the Wind Sing*'s opening page, state something, then immediately qualify or intensify it, is still here, technically. *"The melody confused me, the way it always does"* is followed at once by *"no — this time it shook and confused me far more violently than usual than it ever had before."* The shape of the sentence is the same device Chapter 2 traced back to his debut: a claim, then an italicized correction riding right on top of it. What's changed is the direction that correction runs. In *Hear the Wind Sing*, that rhythm almost always deflated: a big claim, punctured immediately by a flat, dry qualifier. Here, at thirty-seven, on a descending plane, it intensifies instead. The correction pushes toward more feeling, not less. Same architecture, opposite function. A writer who'd built his entire early reputation on undercutting his own sentences had, by 1987, found a way to use the identical mechanism to let one hit harder.

That shift in mode is exactly what made *Norwegian Wood* land so differently at home than anything he'd written before it.

## CHAPTER 8: THE BACKLASH TO THE BESTSELLER

*Norwegian Wood* sold at a scale nothing in Murakami's career had prepared him for, and nothing in Japanese publishing at the time had quite prepared the industry for either: cumulative sales that would eventually pass ten million copies, against a career built, up to that point, on novels Murakami himself has described as selling around a hundred thousand copies apiece. Murakami has since said, plainly, that the experience of that jump left him feeling profoundly isolated, that success on that scale made him feel, in his own words, disliked rather than embraced, even as the book turned him into a genuine mainstream phenomenon rather than a writer with a devoted but contained readership.

Some of that discomfort traces back to a decision Murakami made himself, before a single copy sold: the marketing line "100% a love novel" (100%の恋愛小説) used to promote the book on its release. He wrote that line himself. And he's since said what he'd actually wanted to signal wasn't "love novel" at all, but something closer to "100% a realist novel," a way of marking, plainly, that this book was a deliberate departure from the surreal registers of *A Wild Sheep Chase* and *Hard-Boiled Wonderland and the End of the World*. "Love novel" was the retro, slightly tongue-in-cheek shorthand he reached for instead, and it stuck in a way the more accurate label never got the chance to. Readers and critics who took the tagline at face value found a novel more preoccupied with grief, memory, and the specific damage of a friend's suicide than with anything resembling a conventional romance. The actual relationships in the book are unstable, painful, arbitrary, closer to case studies in loss than to a love story built for easy consumption. The book that made him a household name in Japan is also, on a fair reading of what's actually on the page, one of the more miscategorized bestsellers of his career, and largely because of a label he chose for himself.

That gap between the marketing and the book underneath it fed directly into the tension Chapter 3 already laid out. A novel this commercially enormous, sold under a tagline this reductive, was always going to read to a skeptical literary establishment as further evidence that Murakami belonged more to *taishū bungaku*, mass entertainment, than to the *junbungaku* tradition the Akutagawa jury had already found him wanting against in 1979 and 1980. Where the earlier trilogy and *Hard-Boiled Wonderland* had at least been strange enough, formally, to argue over on literary terms, *Norwegian Wood* was the book that made Murakami a figure the whole country had an opinion about — which is exactly the kind of visibility that tends to work against a writer's standing with an establishment that has historically treated broad popularity itself as faintly suspicious.

Murakami's own response to all of this wasn't to stay and fight the label. He and his wife had already left Japan in October 1986, nearly a year before *Norwegian Wood* was published, settling for a period on Spetses and then Mykonos in Greece before moving on to Italy: a stretch of time he's described as an unusually nomadic period even by his own standards, one spent translating other writers' work and drafting *Norwegian Wood* itself far from Tokyo. He remained abroad through the book's publication and the sales explosion that followed it, a trip that had started as ordinary expatriate restlessness stretching into something that, by his own later account of how the book's success actually felt to him, reads like distance kept on purpose, from a level of fame that had stopped feeling like a reward and started feeling like exposure. The most commercially successful book of his career to that point was also the one that sent him furthest from home: a writer who'd spent nearly a decade being told, by Japan's own literary establishment, that he sounded too foreign, choosing, at the moment he finally became inescapable there, to simply not be in the country for a while.

## CHAPTER 9: VERDICT — WHAT UNTRANSLATED MEANS BY THE END OF ACT ONE

This book opened with a question: is the flat, understated, faintly foreign-sounding voice English-language readers know as "Murakami" something his translators built, or something he built himself, in Japanese, before any of them arrived? By the end of this first act of his career, 1979 to 1987, debut to *Norwegian Wood*, the answer is about as clear as literary history gets. He built it. Deliberately, by his own later account, sentence by sentence, by routing his own first novel through English and back. The jury that read the result in 1979 heard exactly what was there, prose with a foreign fingerprint on it, and simply filed that observation under the wrong heading, as a failure of craft rather than a description of method.

Everything that happened across the next eight years complicates that origin story without overturning it. *Hear the Wind Sing* and its two sequels carried the flat voice forward as a single, consistent register, tied to a Rat who may or may not have been a separated piece of the narrator's own psyche. *Hard-Boiled Wonderland* split that single voice into two, using a pronoun choice most readers would never consciously register, *watashi* against *boku*, to mark a fracture in one mind across two entirely different registers of prose. *Norwegian Wood* then did something that looks, on the surface, like an about-face: real cities, a real airline, a narrator locked into a single unadorned first person, no structural trickery at all. But even there, the same underlying mechanism Chapter 2 first identified, a claim, followed immediately by a correcting beat, was still doing the work, just pointed in the opposite direction, toward more feeling rather than less. The technique never left. Only what it was being used for kept changing.

What also never resolved, across this same stretch, was Japan's own verdict on what to make of any of it. Two Akutagawa nominations came back empty, on the specific grounds that the prose sounded too imported. A juror who dismissed him in 1979 came to respect him by the 1990s; a critic who championed him from the very first magazine interview in 1979 turned skeptical of him by 1997. And the book that finally made him inescapable at home (ten million copies, a tagline he wrote and later disowned, a whole country with an opinion about him) sent him running for Greece and Italy rather than settling the argument in his favor. If anything, it sharpened the oldest version of the complaint: too big to be taken seriously as *junbungaku*, too strange in its earlier surreal registers to be comfortable *taishū bungaku* either. Murakami spent this entire first act of his career being read, at home, as something that didn't quite fit the available categories.

That, it turns out, is the actual throughline connecting the prose technique to the reception history, and it's a tighter connection than it might look like on first pass. A writer who built his own voice out of a deliberate refusal to sound fully at home in his own language spent the same years being read, by his own country's critical establishment, as someone who didn't quite belong to its literary tradition either. The not-quite-belonging wasn't only a stylistic accident that happened to draw institutional suspicion. It was the same condition, showing up twice — once in the sentences themselves, once in how those sentences were received. English-language readers who fell for the flat, faintly displaced voice of the *translated* Murakami were responding, without knowing it, to a writer whose relationship to his own native language had never been entirely settled in the first place. That's not a story translation can take full credit for. It's a story translation simply carried forward, into languages where "not quite sounding native" reads less like a flaw and more like an entire literary identity.

The Murakami who leaves Greece and Italy in the years after *Norwegian Wood* is not yet finished being argued over — not by Japan's critics, and not by his own ambitions for what his fiction could do next. That argument, and the far larger books that came out of it, is where the next volume picks up.

## GLOSSARY

- **Junbungaku (純文学)** — "Pure literature": the Japanese literary tradition organized around artistic and aesthetic ambition, judged on formal control and craft rather than commercial appeal. The Akutagawa Prize is reserved for work in this category. See Chapter 3.
- **Taishū bungaku (大衆文学)** — "Mass" or "popular" literature: commercial, entertainment-oriented fiction, the category the Naoki Prize (Japan's other major fiction award) recognizes. See Chapter 3.
- **The Akutagawa Prize (芥川賞)** — Japan's most prestigious award for new and rising *junbungaku* writers, awarded twice yearly. Murakami was nominated twice (1979, 1980) and won neither time. See Chapters 1 and 3.
- **The Gunzō Prize for New Writers (群像新人文学賞)** — The debut-writer's award from the literary magazine *Gunzō* that *Hear the Wind Sing* won in 1979, distinct from and preceding its Akutagawa nomination. See Chapter 1.
- **The Trilogy of the Rat (鼠三部作)** — The fan- and critic-applied name (not Murakami's own designation) for *Hear the Wind Sing*, *Pinball, 1973*, and *A Wild Sheep Chase*, connected by a shared narrator and his friend "the Rat." See Chapter 5.
- **Watashi (私) / Boku (僕)** — Two of several Japanese first-person pronouns, differing in formality and register. *Hard-Boiled Wonderland and the End of the World* uses each for a different half of its split narrative. See Chapter 6.

## A NOTE ON WHAT COMES NEXT

This is the first of three volumes on Murakami's work. This one ends in 1987, at the height of a domestic sales phenomenon that left its own author feeling more alienated than validated. The second volume picks up from there — the ambitious, historically engaged novels of his middle career, the two national traumas (the Kobe earthquake and the Tokyo subway sarin attack) that pulled his fiction toward direct social engagement, and the question of how one writer's very particular, very private sense of alienation ended up as the default emotional register for an entire generation of readers who'd never heard of the Akutagawa Prize and wouldn't have cared if they had.
