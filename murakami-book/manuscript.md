# MURAKAMI, UNTRANSLATED

### The Voice Japan Doubted and the World Fell For — From a Japanese Researcher to Readers Who Only Know Him in English

*A Japanese Culture Press Book*

This book is an independent work of literary criticism and research about the writer Haruki Murakami and his work. It is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or representing Murakami himself, his publishers (Kodansha, Shinchosha, and others in Japan; Knopf and Vintage in English translation), or any of his translators. ## About the Author

Jiro Naozane is an independent researcher and writer covering Japanese culture — anime, manga, food culture, and now literature — for over a decade, after studying Japanese culture at university, where a student research circle turned casual curiosity into something closer to an ongoing project. This book draws directly on Japanese-language sources — literary-magazine criticism, prize-jury commentary, and Murakami's own Japanese-language essays — that have shaped how he's actually been read and argued about at home, alongside the English-language reception more familiar to this book's readers.

## How to Read This Book

This isn't a plot-summary guide to Murakami's novels, and it isn't a ranked list of which book to read first — plenty of other resources already do that. This is an attempt to answer a specific, underexamined question: why a writer treated with real suspicion by Japan's own literary establishment for most of his early career became the most widely read Japanese novelist alive anywhere else in the world — and whether the voice international readers love was, in a strange and literal sense, built for translation before a single translator ever touched it.

## THE QUESTION THIS WHOLE BOOK IS TRYING TO ANSWER

Is the "Murakami voice" that English-language readers know — flat, understated, faintly foreign-sounding even in translation — something translators created, or something Murakami built into the original Japanese himself, deliberately, as an act of not quite wanting to sound Japanese at all?

## CHAPTER 1: THE PRIZE HE NEVER WON

Haruki Murakami is, by any ordinary measure, one of the most successful novelists alive. His books sell in the millions across dozens of languages, he's been a perennial name in Nobel Prize speculation for over a decade, and outside Japan he functions as something close to a synonym for "contemporary Japanese literature" itself, whether that reputation is fair to the rest of the field or not. It's easy to assume a career like that must have been decorated from the very beginning. It wasn't.

In 1979, a 29-year-old jazz-bar owner named Haruki Murakami published his first novel, *Hear the Wind Sing* (風の歌を聴け), and won the Gunzō Prize for New Writers — a respected debut award from the literary magazine *Gunzō*, and a legitimate credential. On the strength of that novel, he was nominated for the 81st Akutagawa Prize, Japan's most prestigious award for new and rising fiction writers, awarded twice yearly and treated, then as now, as close to a career-making stamp of literary legitimacy. He didn't win. The prize that round went to Yoshiko Shigekane's *Yamaai no Kemuri* and Sō Aono's *Gusha no Yoru*. The following year, his second novel, *Pinball, 1973* (1973年のピンボール), was nominated again. He didn't win that time either — and the jury commentary that survives from that round is considerably less polite than a simple pass-over. One recurring criticism, repeated in various forms across the panel's remarks, was that the prose read as though its author had spent too much time reading foreign novels in translation — 外国翻訳小説の読み過ぎ, roughly "too much reading of foreign translated novels" — offered as a criticism, not a compliment.

It's worth sitting with how strange that specific complaint looks in hindsight. The jury wasn't wrong about what they were hearing. They just read it as a flaw instead of a method — and, as later chapters in this book will get into in some detail, they were reacting to something closer to a genuine technique than they realized: prose that had, in a very literal sense, been passed through English on its way to the page. What Japan's most influential literary prize committee flagged as a young writer's failure to sound sufficiently like himself turned out, decades later, to be one of the more accurate readings anyone gave *Hear the Wind Sing* on its first pass. They just didn't mean it as praise, and Murakami didn't win.

## CHAPTER 2: THE NOVEL WRITTEN BACKWARD

Murakami has told the story of how he actually wrote *Hear the Wind Sing* several times over the years, most substantially in his own essay collection on his development as a writer. The broad shape of the story, as he's described it himself, goes like this: after starting the novel in Japanese and finding the results flat and unconvincing — too close, in his own account, to the kind of "novelistic" Japanese prose he'd grown up reading and didn't want to reproduce — he tried something else. He wrote a portion of it in English instead, deliberately working within the limited vocabulary and simple sentence structures available to him as a non-native English writer, then translated that English back into Japanese himself. He's described this less as a stunt than as a deliberate search for a plainer, more stripped-down Japanese prose style than the literary Japanese he already knew how to write — a way of discovering a new register in his own language by first filtering his own thinking through a language he had less mastery of, and therefore less inherited baggage in.

The effect this produced is more or less exactly what the Akutagawa jury flagged as a weakness a year later: prose with a faint, hard-to-place foreignness to it, even in the original Japanese — short declarative sentences, an understated emotional register, an avoidance of certain dense literary constructions common in the Japanese fiction of the period. It reads, in the original, a little like something that has already been translated once, because in a real sense it has been.

This detail matters for more than trivia value. It means the "Murakami voice" that English-language readers encounter — spare, flat, faintly detached — isn't only a product of what his translators (Alfred Birnbaum first, later Jay Rubin, Philip Gabriel, and Ted Goossen) chose to do with his Japanese. Some of that flatness was already built into the source text, by design, before any of them were involved. A translator rendering Murakami's Japanese into English isn't discovering a foreign text and making it sound natural in English. In a strange, specific way, they're translating something that was partly built by passing through English already — carrying a text back toward a language it had briefly, deliberately, been made to resemble.

**What the Opening Page Actually Does**

It's one thing to describe this technique secondhand. It's another to look at what it actually produces on the page. *Hear the Wind Sing* opens with a flat, aphoristic claim — there's no such thing as perfect writing, the narrator says, the same way there's no such thing as perfect despair — attributed to a writer he says he once knew, left otherwise unnamed. That opening move alone is worth pausing on: a stated universal truth, handed to an anonymous, unspecified source, rather than claimed directly by the narrator himself. It's a device that keeps recurring across the opening page. A throwaway comparison about being able to write something about an elephant but nothing about the man who tends it. A count of years — eight of them — repeated once for emphasis, then closed off with a flat four-word verdict: a long stretch of time. A sweeping claim about aging and learning, immediately followed by the narrator undercutting his own profundity in the next breath, essentially footnoting himself: that's just a generalization.

That rhythm — a statement, then a short, blunt sentence fragment that either restates it or deflates it — repeats often enough in the space of a single page that it starts to look less like a stylistic tic and more like a structural principle. It's a very different rhythm from the longer, more descriptively elaborated sentences that dominated the Japanese literary fiction Murakami would have grown up reading, and it maps unusually well onto the specific American writers he's named, at different points, as formative: Kurt Vonnegut's habit of deadpan authorial intrusion, undercutting his own sentences almost as soon as he's finished them; Richard Brautigan's fondness for planting an odd, specific, faintly absurd image (an elephant, an elephant keeper) directly inside an otherwise abstract passage; the terse, unsentimental declaratives that define Raymond Chandler's prose, a writer Murakami has called one of the most important books he's ever encountered and later translated himself. None of that is coincidence dressed up as pattern-matching. Murakami has named all three as direct influences, and early readers of his debut noticed the American fingerprint immediately, well before anyone had the full story about how the novel was actually assembled.

There's a grammatical detail worth noting too, easy to miss without seeing the actual sentences: the narrator of this opening page is very rarely the subject doing something. He's the object of what happens to him. People arrive, talk at him, and pass over him — the text's own image is of them crossing him the way traffic crosses a bridge, with sound and motion, and then gone for good — while he stays silent, closed-mouthed, letting it happen rather than acting on it. That passivity isn't just a mood the passage describes. It's built into the sentence structure itself, and it's the same passivity critics and readers would spend the next four decades describing as the defining emotional register of nearly every Murakami protagonist who came after him: someone things happen to, narrating from a slight remove, even from his own life.

## CHAPTER 3: WHAT "LIGHT" MEANT AS AN INSULT

To understand why a panel of respected literary judges reacted to *Hear the Wind Sing* the way they did, it helps to understand a distinction that barely exists in English-language book culture but has shaped Japanese literary life for over a century: the line between *junbungaku* (純文学), "pure literature," and *taishū bungaku* (大衆文学), "popular" or "mass" literature. The division traces back to the Meiji and Taishō eras, when the critic and poet Kitamura Tōkoku helped define *junbungaku* as writing organized around aesthetic and artistic formation rather than entertainment or commercial appeal — a definition that, in practice, came to mean fiction judged on the seriousness of its literary ambition, its formal control, and its engagement with the interior life of its characters, as against fiction judged simply on how well it entertained a reader. Japan's two most prominent literary prizes were built directly around this split, and still are: the Akutagawa Prize (芥川賞), the one Murakami was twice nominated for and twice denied, is reserved for *junbungaku*. The Naoki Prize (直木賞), Japan's other major fiction award, is reserved for *taishū bungaku* — genre fiction, entertainment writing, the kind of book built to be read for pleasure rather than studied for craft. An Akutagawa nomination isn't just an award short-list. It's an institutional claim that a given book belongs to the serious side of that divide in the first place.

This is the frame that makes sense of what actually happened at the 81st Akutagawa Prize jury meeting in 1979. Kenzaburō Ōe — at that point already one of Japan's most respected literary novelists, and, fifteen years later, a Nobel laureate himself — reviewed *Hear the Wind Sing* and judged that it skillfully imitated contemporary American fiction without pushing its author toward developing anything of his own, calling the result, in effect, a dead end for both writer and reader rather than a real step forward. Another juror on the same panel, the novelist and critic Saiichi Maruya, made a related but distinct observation: that the book read as heavily shaped by contemporary American fiction, with a certain recognizably Japanese emotional sensibility layered on top of what was, underneath, an American-style novel. Different jurors, converging on the same basic diagnosis: this reads like something translated, or something written by someone who'd been reading in English more than he'd been reading in Japanese. A year later, *Pinball, 1973* drew the same complaint even more bluntly — that its author had clearly been reading too much foreign fiction in translation.

None of this was small-minded gatekeeping for its own sake. Within the *junbungaku* tradition these jurors were operating in, "sounds like a translation" was a genuinely serious charge, because the tradition itself was built, in large part, around cultivating a distinctly Japanese literary voice — one that had spent the better part of a century defining itself partly in relation to, and partly in resistance against, the flood of Western literature that arrived in Japan from the Meiji era onward. A young novelist whose prose read as though it had been filtered through English wasn't just breaking a stylistic convention. He was, in the eyes of jurors steeped in that tradition, declining to do the specific kind of work *junbungaku* existed to reward. What none of them had access to yet, of course, was the actual explanation Murakami would only lay out himself years later, in his own essays: that the "translated" quality they were hearing wasn't an accident of influence. It was assembled, on purpose, sentence by sentence, by a young writer trying to build a new voice by deliberately routing his own Japanese through a language he barely knew.

## CHAPTER 4: THE CRITICS WHO CHANGED THEIR MINDS — IN BOTH DIRECTIONS

It would be tidier, for a book built around a thesis like this one's, if Japan's literary establishment had simply doubted Murakami from the start and never really come around. That's not what happened, and it's worth resisting the tidier version, because the real story is more interesting and considerably less flattering to any single narrative — including this book's own.

Take Kenzaburō Ōe, the same juror whose 1979 Akutagawa comments dismissed *Hear the Wind Sing* as skillful imitation leading nowhere. Ōe went on, fifteen years later, to win the Nobel Prize in Literature himself, becoming — alongside Yasunari Kawabata a quarter-century earlier — one of only two Japanese laureates in the prize's history at that point. In the years that followed his own Nobel win, Ōe's public relationship to Murakami's work softened considerably; by later in his career, he was willing to credit Murakami's fiction with reaching and holding a genuinely wide readership, treating that reach as something worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as evidence of shallowness. The two men, by most accounts, developed a measure of mutual professional respect that would have been hard to predict from the 81st Akutagawa Prize jury notes alone — helped, it's worth noting, by the fact that Murakami had reportedly been reading Ōe's own work since high school and considered him one of the defining Japanese novelists of the postwar period, well before either man's relationship to the other's work became public literary history.

Now take the opposite trajectory: the critic Saburō Kawamoto. Where Ōe started skeptical and warmed, Kawamoto started as one of Murakami's earliest and most attentive champions and grew more openly critical over time. Kawamoto conducted what's widely cited as the first substantial magazine interview with Murakami, in the literary journal *Cahier* in August 1979 — months after *Hear the Wind Sing*'s debut, when Murakami was still a virtual unknown to most of the literary press. Six years later, in 1985, the two collaborated directly, co-authoring a book together, *Eiga wo Meguru Bōken* ("Adventures Concerning Film"). This wasn't a critic keeping a wary, professional distance. This was someone who'd staked an early, public bet on Murakami being worth serious attention, and had the working relationship to show for it.

That relationship soured, at least in print, over what Kawamoto read as a genuine change in Murakami's own project. Reviewing *Underground* — Murakami's 1997 nonfiction book of interviews with survivors of the 1995 Tokyo subway sarin attack, a sharp turn toward direct social engagement after two decades of interior, often surreal fiction — Kawamoto wrote that he couldn't shake a persistent unease throughout the book, wondering why Murakami had suddenly become what Kawamoto called a "shakai-ha" writer, a socially engaged one, and openly questioned whether Murakami himself had simply fallen into telling a familiar, expected kind of story rather than doing anything genuinely new with the material. It's a sharper break than it might look at first: the critic who'd done the first real interview, who'd co-written a book with the man, essentially accusing him of coasting on a predictable narrative shape at exactly the moment Murakami was trying something outside his established mode entirely.

Put the two trajectories side by side and the actual picture is more honest than either extreme: Japan's critical relationship to Murakami was never a single, static verdict, in either direction. It moved. A juror who once dismissed him as derivative ended up respecting the size and durability of his readership. A critic who once championed him early ended up publicly doubting his motives at the exact moment Murakami tried to prove he was more than a genre writer working the same groove. What stayed constant, on both sides of that shifting critical weather, was that nobody in Japan's literary press was treating Murakami the way most of his English-language readers eventually would: as a settled, uncomplicated literary giant. He was — and largely still is, in Japan — an argument that's never quite finished.
