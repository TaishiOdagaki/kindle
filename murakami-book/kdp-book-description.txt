<p><b>Zero. That's how many times Japan's most prestigious literary prize ever went to Haruki Murakami</b> — the writer who'd go on to sell in the millions, in dozens of languages, and become the default answer to "name a famous living Japanese novelist" for readers who've never heard of the Akutagawa Prize at all.</p>

<p>Murakami's own country's most serious literary judges spent years insisting his prose sounded foreign — and they weren't simply wrong. This book traces the deliberate technique behind that "foreign" voice (he drafted his debut novel in English first, then hand-translated it back into Japanese) and the decade of domestic rejection and uneasy triumph that followed, from a 1978 afternoon at a Tokyo ballpark through the sales phenomenon that finally made him inescapable at home — and sent him running to Greece and Italy instead of enjoying it. Written by a bilingual researcher who works from the original Japanese-language jury records, criticism, and essays most English-language coverage never reaches.</p>

<h4>What's inside:</h4>
<ul>
<li>The real, verified story behind Murakami's "foreign" voice: drafting his debut novel in English, then hand-translating it back into Japanese — the technique this entire book is built around</li>
<li>The actual 1979 and 1980 Akutagawa Prize jury remarks, sourced directly to the original prize records — including a popular myth this book corrects on the page: what Kenzaburō Ōe is widely believed to have said about Murakami's debut, versus what he's actually on record saying</li>
<li>A close reading of the "Trilogy of the Rat" and <i>Hard-Boiled Wonderland and the End of the World</i>'s split narration — one mind, two pronouns, two entirely different registers — quoted from both the original Japanese and the published English translations</li>
<li>Why <i>Norwegian Wood</i> becoming an unignorable, ten-million-copy phenomenon in Japan sent its own author further from home, not closer to it</li>
<li>A verdict chapter that takes an actual position on what "the Murakami voice" really is, rather than surveying everyone else's opinion</li>
</ul>

<p>Every specific claim, date, and quotation in this book was checked against a primary source before it was written down — original prize-jury remarks, first-edition Japanese texts, and published English translations, not secondhand summaries of them.</p>
