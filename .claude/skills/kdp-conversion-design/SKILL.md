# Interior & Front-Matter Design for Conversion

This skill exists because ad diagnosis (see `kdp-ads-strategy`'s 2026-09-28 entries) pointed at the listing itself, not ad targeting: Chainsaw Man and Demon Slayer both proved real, healthy click-through at a moment when both franchises were near or at all-time US mainstream-attention peaks, and still converted at zero — meaning the bottleneck was the page a clicker actually landed on, not the ad. Two separate reader objections were named as the target: **text fatigue** (a wall of prose with nothing to look at) and **"is this AI-written?" suspicion**. Everything below is the response to those two objections, plus general Kindle "Look Inside" conversion research. All of it currently lives in three sibling anime/manga companion books (Chainsaw Man, Demon Slayer, Attack on Titan) under the Jiro Naozane pen name.

## Interior visual design system

- **Chapter-opener images, one per Intro/chapter, built from the book's own cover photo.** Take the actual cover art (a real product photograph — a chainsaw, a sword hilt, a cracked wall — not stock art or generic clip-art), crop to an interesting detail, convert to grayscale, and bake the chapter title directly into the image with Pillow (`ImageDraw`/`ImageFont`, Liberation Sans Bold). Reusing the same real photograph across every chapter opener (not 15 different unique illustrations) is deliberate — it's what real published nonfiction does for chapter dividers, it's far less production effort, and it ties every chapter visually back to the cover.
  - **Why baked-in text, not CSS-overlaid text:** Kindle's reflowable EPUB format does not reliably support precisely-positioned text-over-image layering (`position: absolute` and similar) across devices — it renders inconsistently or breaks outright on e-ink readers specifically. Fixed-layout EPUB would fix this but forces the whole book out of reflowable text, wrong for a text-heavy nonfiction book. Baking the text into the image with Pillow guarantees identical rendering everywhere, at the cost of the title no longer being reflowable/selectable text — an acceptable trade for a decorative divider page, not for body content.
  - **Links work fine on Kindle**, just not combined with precise positioning: a whole-image link or an ordinary in-flow text link both render reliably. Only "text in an exact position on top of an image, and also a live link" hits the same reliability wall as the CSS-overlay problem.
- **Data-visualization charts grounded in the book's own specific argument, never generic infographics.** The test: could this exact chart exist for a different book with only the labels swapped? If yes, it's decorative and reads as AI-slop; if no — it required actually reading and understanding this book's own text — it's doing real work. Concrete examples built so far: a "leash" diagram literalizing a chapter's own repeated leash/collar metaphor with the specific named characters it's run on (not a generic relationship map); a fear-taxonomy spectrum plotting the story's own named monsters; a two-column comparison pulled verbatim from one chapter's own contrast (ordinary shonen-hero wants vs. this protagonist's wants); a kanji-meaning breakdown of a character's actual name; a concentric-circle map of a story's own three-tier walled geography; a cast-name etymology table; verified publication/adaptation timelines (every date fact-checked via WebSearch before use, never guessed); box-office bar charts with real, sourced figures.
- **One consistent accent color per book, shared house style otherwise.** White background, black bold Liberation Sans headlines, a single accent color used for labels/icons/bars. Each book in the series gets its own accent (Chainsaw Man: orange, matching its cover; Demon Slayer: steel-blue; Attack on Titan: muted brick-red) so the series reads as one system without every book looking identical.
- **A front-matter "WHAT'S INSIDE" chapter roadmap, spoiler-tagged, placed to land inside the Amazon preview sample.** This single asset does double duty: it's a strong, immediate "this book was actually designed" signal (the anti-AI-slop objection), and it directly defuses the spoiler-anxious anime-only reader's biggest purchase hesitation by showing exactly how far they can read safely, before they've spent a dollar. Reuse the same chapter-title list as the back-matter Spoiler Map, just styled as a front-facing hook rather than a reference table.
- **PNG for flat-color/text charts, JPEG for anything photographic.** PNG compresses flat vector-style graphics (charts, icons, text) excellently and photographic content terribly — chapter-opener images saved as PNG bloated one book's EPUB to 31MB (over the file-delivery limit this session hit); converting those same images to JPEG (quality ~87) cut total size by ~77% with no visible quality loss, while the charts stayed PNG. Match the codec to the content type, don't default to one format for everything.
- **Pandoc `--split-level=2` gotcha: image/heading order determines whether the EPUB's table of contents actually reaches the image.** Pandoc splits the EPUB into one XHTML file per level-2 heading, splitting exactly at the heading boundary. An image placed *before* a `## CHAPTER N` heading in the markdown lands at the tail of the *previous* chapter's file, not the start of its own — so a reader clicking that chapter's TOC link jumps straight past the image to the plain-text heading, never seeing it. **Always place a chapter-opener (or any hero image meant to represent a heading) immediately *after* that heading, never before it.** Verify by unzipping the built EPUB and confirming the heading and image share one `EPUB/text/chNNN.xhtml` file.

## Front-matter strategy: what research says, and what this project did about it

Sourced from KDP's own help pages and multiple indie-publishing craft blogs (see the 2026-09-28 log entry below for the specific search queries and sources).

- **Amazon's "Look Inside" preview and the post-purchase "Start Reading Location" are different things, and conflating them is the single most common front-matter mistake.** Look Inside shows the sample (10% of the book by default) completely unabridged, front matter included, to a browsing, not-yet-paying reader. Start Reading Location is where a *purchased* copy opens, and Amazon's system skips front matter to drop a paying reader straight into real content. **Every front-matter section therefore has a real cost: it eats into the free-preview budget a potential buyer sees, competing directly against the actual chapters that would otherwise be doing the selling.** Front matter that's easy to justify in isolation ("readers like knowing about the author," "the tag system needs explaining") still needs to earn its place against this cost.
- **The Look Inside sample percentage is adjustable** (5%–40% in 5% steps for ebooks, via KDP). If front matter can't be trimmed further, raising this is the other lever — not yet used on any of this project's books, worth considering if front matter needs to stay long for a specific reason.
- **Nonfiction hooks should lead with the reader's own stakes or desire, not the author's credentials or the book's plot.** Applied as "A Note Before You Begin" — book-specific, written to make someone want to keep reading, not a boilerplate description.
- **A short credibility signal (who wrote this, why they're worth trusting) belongs early — and the user's own call, after trying it folded into the hook, was that it's worth its own short heading.** First tried as a single closing sentence of "A Note Before You Begin" (cutting a whole section); reverted the same day on the user's explicit reasoning that a clearly-labeled "ABOUT THE AUTHOR" heading is itself part of the anti-AI-slop trust signal — "a Japanese researcher wrote this" reads as more credible with its own heading than folded into hook prose, worth the small added front-matter cost. Current standing version: its own heading, kept to two sentences.
- **A detailed table of contents can itself be a deciding factor for a nonfiction sale** — consistent with the WHAT'S INSIDE chart above; no separate action needed, just don't cut it when trimming.
- **Don't repeat the same setup in two different front-matter sections.** Caught concretely in Demon Slayer: "How to Read This Book" opened by restating almost verbatim the box-office hook already made in the very next section ("The Question This Whole Book Is Trying to Answer"). Cutting the duplicate lost nothing.
- **General front-matter order that's now standard across all three books in this series:** title page → **A Note Before You Begin** (book-specific hook only) → **About the Author** (its own short heading, ~2 sentences, leads with "a Japanese researcher and critic") → **How to Read This Book** (WHAT'S INSIDE chart + a tightly-trimmed spoiler-tag legend, cut down to definitions and a Spoiler Map pointer only — no extended philosophy-of-spoilers essay) → Introduction/Chapter 1. The rights-holder disclaimer lives at the very end of the book (back matter), not the front — it's a legal notice, not a hook, and costs nothing being read last by someone who already bought the book.

## Standing process rule

**Do not rebuild or send an EPUB until the user explicitly asks for it.** Manuscript edits, chart/image generation, and commits can all happen freely; the `pandoc` build + `epubcheck` + send-to-user step waits for an explicit go-ahead, since the user reviews text/structure changes first and may want another round before spending a build.
