### 1988–2010: The Decade His Loneliness Became Everyone Else's

## How to Read This Book

A note on who this is for: you don't need Volume One in hand to follow this one — the parts of the earlier argument that matter get recapped as they come up — but if you've read it, you'll see exactly where this book picks up the thread. Either way, you're in the right place.

Volume One left Murakami in 1987: newly famous at home, and privately unsettled by what that fame actually felt like. This volume picks up from there and follows the stretch of his career that made him strange in a new way — formally ambitious, historically serious, willing to sit for months across a table from survivors of a sarin gas attack and just listen. Two national catastrophes reshape what his fiction is for. A private, particular kind of loneliness, his own at first, spreads out until it's something closer to a generation's default emotional weather, in Japan and then well beyond it. That's the arc this volume traces: not a bigger Murakami, exactly, but a Murakami whose smallest, most personal instinct turned out to scale.

Let's get into it.

---

## THE QUESTION THIS VOLUME IS TRYING TO ANSWER

How does one writer's private, specific loneliness end up as an entire generation's emotional shorthand, in Japan first and then well beyond it? And what did he actually have to do, as a novelist and not as a phenomenon, to make that happen? This volume answers both, in order — and leaves enough of the argument exposed, chapter by chapter, that you can push back on it before the verdict chapter tries to close it.

## CHAPTER 1: THE SEQUEL NOBODY EXPECTED

**A Sequel Six Years Late**

*A Wild Sheep Chase* had ended with the narrator's closest friend dead, and Murakami had said nothing about him since. Six years is a long time to leave a body unaccounted for, and when Murakami finally did go back for it, readers expecting a straightforward continuation of the Rat trilogy got something considerably stranger. *Dance Dance Dance* (ダンス・ダンス・ダンス), published in October 1988,[^1] is a strange kind of sequel. The narrator picking the story back up is recognizably the same *boku* from the earlier trilogy, older now, still haunted by the same hotel, the same sheep-obsessed conspiracy, and the same friend, the Rat, who by the end of *A Wild Sheep Chase* was already dead. Some readers and critics group all four books together as a loose "Sheep" tetralogy rather than treating the earlier three as a closed trilogy with this one bolted on afterward; either way, this is the book where Murakami went back to close a door he'd left open six years earlier, in a considerably different register than the one he'd left it in.

The tone has shifted along with the years. Where *A Wild Sheep Chase* built its plot around a dense, heavy political allegory, *Dance Dance Dance* is a more polished, psychologically interior novel about a specific kind of late-1980s emptiness: a freelance writer drifting through Tokyo's hyper-consumerist boom years, doing hollow corporate work he doesn't believe in, entering a city that's been renovated past recognition since he last paid attention to it. The Rat is gone. The friend who anchored the earlier trilogy's structure, whose parallel chapters and possible status as a split-off piece of the narrator's own psyche drove so much of *Pinball, 1973*'s formal experiment, is simply absent from this book, present only as something the narrator has to learn to live without. In his place is the Sheep Man (羊男), a recurring figure from *A Wild Sheep Chase* who returns here as something closer to a guide through grief than a plot device.

**"Dance for as Long as the Music Plays"**

The Sheep Man's advice to the narrator isn't delivered as comfort; it's delivered as something closer to an operating instruction, repeated and insisted on until it stops sounding like metaphor. In the original Japanese: 「踊るんだよ」「音楽の鳴っている間はとにかく踊り続けるんだ。おいらの言っていることはわかるかい？踊るんだ。踊り続けるんだ。何故踊るかなんて考えちゃいけない。意味なんてことは考えちゃいけない。意味なんてもともとないんだ。そんなこと考えだしたら足が停まる。一度足が停まったら、もうおいらには何ともしてあげられなくなってしまう」— roughly: dance, he says; as long as the music's playing, just keep dancing, do you understand me, dance, keep dancing, don't think about why you're dancing, don't think about meaning, there wasn't any meaning to begin with, start thinking like that and your feet stop, and once your feet stop there's nothing more he can do for you.[^2] The speech ends the way it started, stripped down to the bare instruction with everything else worn away: 「だから踊るんだよ。音楽の続く限り」— so dance, he tells him. For as long as the music plays.[^2] It's advice built for a novel about a man who has nothing left to organize his life around except grief for a friend the book has already declined to bring back; keep moving isn't offered as healing, just as the only alternative to stopping.

**Closing a Door Rather Than Reopening One**

It's a quieter, sadder kind of sequel than the word usually implies. Murakami isn't returning to the Rat trilogy to extend its world for its own sake. He's returning to it to formally close the door on the particular, contained loneliness that voice had carried since 1979, and to ask what a person who's outlived that version of himself is actually supposed to do next. The rest of this volume is largely the story of him answering that question with increasingly larger, more historically ambitious books, but *Dance Dance Dance* is where he first admits, on the page, that simply repeating the earlier formula wasn't an option anymore.

[^2]: Murakami Haruki, *Dance Dance Dance* [ダンス・ダンス・ダンス] (Tokyo: Kōdansha Bunko, 1991); the Sheep Man's speech quoted directly from the user's copy.

## CHAPTER 2: THE NOVEL THAT CONVINCED THE SKEPTICS

**A Novel That Stopped Being Arguable**

Three parts. Three years to write. One buried scene unflinching enough to close an argument that had followed Murakami since 1979. *The Wind-Up Bird Chronicle* (ねじまき鳥クロニクル), published across three parts between 1994 and 1995, is where his fiction stopped being arguable on the "too foreign, too light" terms that had followed him since the Akutagawa jury notes. It opens with one of the most recognizable first lines in modern Japanese fiction: "The phone rang while I was boiling spaghetti in the kitchen" (台所でスパゲティーをゆでているときに、電話がかかってきた).[^7]

The novel's contemporary plot, an unemployed man searching Tokyo for his missing wife and cat, keeps folding back into a lengthy, unflinching first-person account of the Nomonhan Incident: the brutal, poorly documented 1939 border war between Japanese and Soviet-Mongolian forces in Manchuria,[^3] narrated through the memory of a former Kwantung Army officer named Lieutenant Mamiya. The violence in that account is not softened or stylized. Mamiya's own narration makes the absence of any real guiding principle explicit rather than implied: reflecting on the war years, he recalls wondering why he had to risk his life defending land with "no value, industrially or militarily," land that could barely support a stand of crops, and calling the whole demand "utterly absurd" (馬鹿げたこと) even as he went on fighting anyway.[^6] It's a description considerably more pointed than anything Murakami's earlier fiction had attempted about Japan's own wartime history.

**Research Done in the Wrong Order**

The research underneath that section was real, not simply invented from atmosphere and secondhand reading, and it happened in an unusual order. Murakami began serializing the novel's first part in late 1992, and it was only after that first installment had already appeared in print that the magazine *Marco Polo* encouraged him to go see the actual ground his fiction was describing. He traveled to the Nomonhan battlefield itself in June 1994, crossing into the region via both Chinese and Mongolian routes, and wrote an account of that trip that ran in *Marco Polo* before being collected in his essay volume *Henkyō, Kinkyō* ("Borderland and Nearby").[^4] This wasn't a novelist borrowing the general shape of a historical event for texture from his desk. It was a writer who'd already started publishing the fiction going out afterward to stand on the actual battlefield, the kind of primary-source engagement Japan's own *junbungaku* tradition, the same tradition that had spent 1979 and 1980 accusing him of reading too much foreign fiction and not enough of his own country's history, would have recognized immediately as serious.

**The Novel Shiba Ryōtarō Couldn't Write**

There's a specific, almost uncanny detail worth sitting with here. Shiba Ryōtarō, one of the most respected historical novelists in modern Japanese literature, spent years researching the Nomonhan Incident himself, hoping to turn it into a major work, before ultimately abandoning the project: by his own account, the deeper he went into the material, the more despairing and directionless it left him, to the point of saying the research made him feel he couldn't understand his own country.[^5] He never published a novel out of that research. Murakami, a writer whose own literary establishment had spent fifteen years treating him as insufficiently serious to be trusted with exactly this kind of material, went ahead and wrote it anyway, folding it into a novel that also features a psychic prostitute, a man who skins another man alive, and a protagonist who spends long stretches of the book sitting at the bottom of a dry well. *The Wind-Up Bird Chronicle* didn't resolve the *junbungaku* question by abandoning the surreal, private, interior fiction that had drawn suspicion in the first place. It resolved it by proving that register could hold real historical weight without setting any of its strangeness aside.

## CHAPTER 3: TWO EARTHQUAKES

**Two Catastrophes, Ten Weeks Apart**

Japan absorbed two catastrophes within ten weeks of each other in 1995. On January 17, the Great Hanshin earthquake killed more than six thousand people in and around Kobe. On March 20, members of the Aum Shinrikyo cult released sarin gas on five separate Tokyo subway trains during the morning rush hour, killing thirteen and injuring thousands more.[^8] Murakami, still living partly abroad at the time, has described a growing pull back toward wanting to understand "Japan as a country" after years of what he's called his own exile, and it was the second of these two events that pulled him into the most sustained piece of nonfiction he'd ever attempt.

**Refusing the Faceless Victim**

*Underground* (アンダーグラウンド), published in 1997,[^9] is built almost entirely out of interviews: with sarin attack survivors, with the rescue workers and station staff who responded that morning, and, notably, with former Aum members themselves, included specifically so the book wouldn't reduce the event to a single, simple villain's-eye account. Murakami has explained the project's core motivation plainly: he didn't want the people he was interviewing to disappear into the anonymous mass of "one of many faceless victims," a phrase he's used himself to describe exactly the flattening he was trying to write against. It's his first full-length work of nonfiction, and it required a kind of patient, unglamorous listening that his fiction, for all its formal ambition, had never demanded of him before.

**The Other Half of the Job**

Three years later, *after the quake* (神の子どもたちはみな踊る), published in February 2000,[^10] took up the other half of that same year. The collection, serialized in the magazine *Shinchō* under the working heading "After the Earthquake," gathers six stories whose characters are all touched, indirectly rather than head-on, by the Kobe earthquake, none of them physically caught in the disaster itself, all of them somehow rearranged by it anyway. In his own afterword to the collection, Murakami said plainly that once he'd finished dealing with the sarin attack and Aum, he found he wanted to write about the Kobe earthquake too, and that addressing only one of the two events would have felt, in his own words, lopsided, unbalanced, like doing only half the job.

Taken together, the two books mark the clearest turn Murakami's fiction had made since his debut: away from the private, interior, largely apolitical register of the Rat trilogy and toward direct engagement with events that had actually happened to actual people, on a specific date, with a body count. This is the same shift Saburō Kawamoto flagged with suspicion in Volume One of this series, questioning whether Murakami had simply become a "shakai-ha," a socially engaged writer, chasing an expected kind of story rather than doing anything genuinely his own with the material. Whatever the fairness of that specific complaint, the two 1995 catastrophes mark the point after which Murakami's fiction stopped being arguable as a writer working entirely inside his own head. He had gone out into the country whose literary establishment had spent two decades calling him insufficiently Japanese, and started listening to it directly.

## CHAPTER 4: KAFKA ON THE SHORE AND THE EXPORT OF A STYLE

**A Novel Mysterious Even at Home**

Eight thousand. That's roughly how many confused reader questions Murakami's own Japanese publisher fielded through a website it built for exactly this purpose — not from readers struggling with a foreign text in translation, but from readers already fluent in Japanese, already fluent in Murakami. *Kafka on the Shore* (海辺のカフカ) appeared in Japan in 2002, and that number is the small, telling detail about just how dense and genuinely puzzling the book was, even at home. This wasn't a simple story that needed translation to become mysterious. It arrived mysterious, at home, in the original.

**A Storm That Turns Out to Be Yourself**

The novel gives its own instructions early on for how not to go looking for a solution, in a passage that reads, in hindsight, almost like it's addressed to those eight thousand confused readers before a single one of them had written in. In the original Japanese: 「ある場合には運命というのは、絶えまなく進行方向を変える局地的な砂嵐に似ている。君はそれを避けようと足取りを変える。そうすると嵐も君に合わせるように足取りを変える」— roughly: sometimes fate is like a localized sandstorm that keeps shifting the direction it's moving in; you change your own path to avoid it, and the storm shifts to match you. The passage doesn't let the metaphor stay comfortably external for long: 「それが繰り返される。なぜかと言えば、その嵐はどこか遠くからやってきた無関係な何かじゃないからだ。そいつはつまり君自身のことなんだ。君の中にある何かなんだ」— that keeps happening, precisely because the storm was never some unrelated thing that arrived from somewhere far away. It's you. Something inside you.[^11] The only advice on offer isn't escape, it's endurance: 「だから君にできることといえばあきらめてその嵐の中にまっすぐ足を踏み入れ、砂が入らないように目と耳をしっかりふさぎ一歩一歩通り抜けていくことだけだ」— all you can do is give up, walk straight into the storm, shut your eyes and ears tight against the sand, and go through it one step at a time.[^11] It's strange advice to hand a fifteen-year-old runaway at the start of his own novel, and it doubles, by the book's own reception, as a fair warning to the reader too: this isn't a puzzle with a solution waiting on the other side of it. It's weather made out of yourself, that you're only ever going to walk through.

**The Translation Accelerates Everything**

The English translation, by Philip Gabriel, followed in January 2005, and the speed and scale of its reception marked a real acceleration from anything his earlier work had managed abroad. The New York Times named it one of its ten best books of the year.[^12] The following year, it won the World Fantasy Award for best novel,[^12] a recognition from within the English-language genre-fiction world specifically, not just the more general literary press, suggesting his work had started to find an audience well beyond readers who'd come to him through Japanese-literature-in-translation circles alone. His body of work, cumulatively, has since been translated into some fifty languages, a figure that includes but isn't limited to this specific period of acceleration. A novel this strange, this genuinely difficult to summarize even for its most devoted domestic readers, was somehow also becoming one of the more reliably ported literary exports Japan had ever produced.

[^11]: Murakami Haruki, *Kafka on the Shore* [海辺のカフカ] (Tokyo: Shinchōsha Bunko, 2002); the sandstorm passage quoted directly from the user's copy.
[^12]: *Kafka on the Shore*, translated by Philip Gabriel, was named one of *The New York Times*' ten best books of 2005 and won the 2006 World Fantasy Award for Best Novel.

That combination, formally difficult at home and increasingly frictionless abroad, is worth sitting with rather than treating as a simple contradiction. The same qualities Japan's literary establishment had spent over two decades treating with suspicion (the flatness, the foreignness, the refusal to resolve into tidy realist meaning) were, by the mid-2000s, exactly what an international readership had learned to read as distinctively, recognizably *his*, rather than as evidence he didn't belong to any tradition cleanly. A style built out of an act of not-quite-belonging had found, abroad, an audience that didn't need him to belong to anything in particular at all.

## CHAPTER 5: THE EVENT NOVEL

**A Launch Treated Like a National Event**

Two volumes. One release date. A fourth printing confirmed before the release day was even over. *1Q84*, released simultaneously on May 29, 2009, is where Murakami's publishing career stopped looking like a writer's career and started looking, briefly, like a national event with a release date. The book was large even by his own standards: a genuinely massive, structurally ambitious novel alternating between two narrators in a Tokyo running one digit off from the one readers actually lived in, threaded through with a cult, a rewritten fairy tale, and two small moons hanging in a sky where there should only be one.

Its publisher, Shinchōsha, reportedly floated the possibility beforehand that the release could become something close to a social phenomenon rather than an ordinary book launch, and the sales figures that followed backed that framing up quickly: combined initial print runs across the two volumes reached roughly 680,000 copies.[^13] Advance sales in the two days before the nationwide release, limited to the Tokyo metropolitan area and the Kansai region, are estimated at around 100,000 copies on their own.[^13] The novel went on to win the Mainichi Publishing Culture Award and generated what contemporary coverage described, without much exaggeration, as an unprecedented level of attention both domestically and internationally.

**From a Hundred Thousand Copies to a National Occasion**

What makes *1Q84* worth treating as a distinct kind of event, rather than simply Murakami's biggest bestseller since *Norwegian Wood*, is the shape that attention actually took. This wasn't a quiet, gradually accumulating readership discovering a book over years, the way his early trilogy had built its audience. It was a coordinated, closely watched release, anticipated in advance, tracked in sales figures within days rather than seasons, treated by his publisher as an event to be managed rather than simply a book to be shipped. The Murakami whose early novels sold, by his own account, around a hundred thousand copies apiece, and whose 1987 breakout left him keeping distance from his own country's attention rather than embracing it, had become, twenty-two years later, someone whose publisher could reasonably plan for a launch on the scale of a genuine cultural occasion, and be right to.

**An Opening Built Around Not Quite Recognizing Something**

Before any of that scale becomes visible, the novel opens small and specific: a woman named Aomame, sitting in the back of a taxi stuck in traffic, half-listening to a classical station playing on the radio. The chapter itself is subtitled "Don't Let Appearances Fool You" (見かけにだまされないように), and the opening page immediately puts that warning to work on something as minor as a piece of music.[^14] The taxi driver, absorbed in reading the traffic ahead "like a seasoned fisherman reading a bad tide," barely seems to register the piece playing; Aomame does, leaning back with her eyes half-closed. In the original Japanese: 「ヤナーチェックの『シンフォニエッタ』の冒頭部分を耳にして、これはヤナーチェックの『シンフォニエッタ』だと言い当てられる人が、世間にいったいどれくらいいるだろうか。おそらく「とても少ない」と「ほとんどいない」の中間くらいではあるだろう」 — roughly, in translation: hearing just the opening bars of Janáček's *Sinfonietta* and correctly naming it is something vanishingly few people could actually manage; probably somewhere between "very few" and "almost none." It's a small, almost throwaway piece of musical trivia to open a six-hundred-page novel on, and that's exactly the point: a detail this minor, this easy to miss or misread, turns out to be the reader's first cue that the world on the page isn't quite the one it looks like at a glance, the same warning the chapter's own subtitle already gave before a single line of prose.

[^14]: Murakami Haruki, *1Q84*, BOOK 1 (Tokyo: Shinchōsha, 2009), Chapter 1 ("青豆," subtitled "見かけにだまされないように"), p. 11.

## CHAPTER 6: THE WRITERS WHO READ HIM FIRST

**Murakami Haruki Children**

By the 2000s, Murakami's influence on other Japanese writers had become visible enough to earn its own shorthand. Novelists Takayuki Honda and Yoshio Ōsaki are among the writers most often described, in Japanese literary commentary, as part of a loose group nicknamed "Murakami Haruki Children" (村上春樹チルドレン),[^15] a label pointing to recognizable traces of Murakami's prose habits, metaphor style, and recurring motifs showing up in a younger generation's own fiction: in Ōsaki's case, a similarly spare, melancholic prose built around loss and quiet recovery. It's a slightly reductive label, the kind critics reach for when a stylistic fingerprint becomes common enough to name, but the underlying observation, that Murakami's particular way of building a sentence had become something other writers were visibly working with or against, is a real one.

**Beyond the Page**

The influence wasn't confined to prose fiction. The animation director Makoto Shinkai has said directly, in magazine interviews, that Murakami's work shaped his own.[^16] It's worth pausing on how far that influence actually traveled. A writer whose entire early reputation rested on sounding too foreign for serious Japanese literature ended up shaping one of the defining animation directors of a later generation — different medium, same emotionally spare, quietly melancholic register, decades in the building.

[^15]: The "Murakami Haruki Children" (村上春樹チルドレン) label, applied to novelists including Takayuki Honda and Yoshio Ōsaki, appears throughout Japanese literary commentary on Murakami's domestic influence.
[^16]: Director Makoto Shinkai has discussed Murakami's influence on his own work in Japanese magazine interviews.

None of this is to claim Murakami invented contemporary Japanese literary sensibility single-handedly, and this book won't pretend the influence runs in only one direction or that every writer working in a Murakami-adjacent register owes him a direct debt. But by the time *1Q84* had turned into a scheduled national event, the writer whose own literary establishment had spent two decades questioning whether his prose belonged to any recognizable Japanese tradition had become, for a younger generation of writers and artists, something closer to a tradition of his own.

## CHAPTER 7: A GENERATION'S DEFAULT MODE

**Naming His Own Terms**

Murakami has his own name for the emotional stance running through his early and mid-career fiction, and it isn't a term this book invented to describe him after the fact. In a published dialogue with the psychologist Hayao Kawai, Murakami traced what he called "detachment" (デタッチメント) directly back to his own disillusionment with the failed student protest movements of the 1960s, the collapse of that generation's political idealism leaving behind, in his account, a deliberate ethical distance from ideology itself, whether the Marxist politics that had just failed or the consumer, postmodern society that replaced it.[^17] His characters hold themselves apart from every system on offer, not out of apathy, but as a considered response to watching one grand narrative after another curdle into something that got people killed.

**A Reframing Not Everyone Accepts**

That stance is exactly what this volume has already traced turning, somewhere around 1995, into something Murakami and his critics both started calling "commitment" (コミットメント) instead: a shift he's connected partly to his own years living abroad, and one this book's own Chapter 3 locates precisely at the two national catastrophes that pulled *Underground* and *after the quake* out of him. It's worth being honest that this reframing isn't universally accepted even among Murakami's most serious readers. The critic Norihiro Katō has specifically pushed back on calling Murakami "a writer of detachment" at all, arguing the label oversimplifies even his earliest work.[^18] This book doesn't need to resolve that specific critical dispute to make its own point, which is narrower: whatever the most precise label, an entire generation of readers, in Japan first and increasingly outside it, learned to recognize their own drift, alienation, and quiet refusal to fully buy into any available system of meaning in Murakami's characters, well before most of them had ever heard the words *detachment* or *commitment* used to describe it critically. The private vocabulary of one writer's disillusionment with a failed 1960s political movement became, somehow, an entire later generation's default emotional register. Most of them never knew where it came from.

## CHAPTER 8: VERDICT — WHAT "EVERYWHERE" COST HIM

**What He Had to Stop Being**

This volume opened with a question: how did one writer's very specific, private sense of loneliness end up as an entire generation's default emotional shorthand, and what did he actually have to do to make that happen? The honest answer, by 2010, is that he had to stop being only one kind of writer. The Murakami who closed out the Rat trilogy's unfinished business in *Dance Dance Dance*, researched a historical atrocity thoroughly enough to succeed where Shiba Ryōtarō had given up, sat across from sarin attack survivors and Aum defectors long enough to write *Underground*, and then let a single novel become a scheduled national event, is not the same figure this series' first volume left running from his own fame in Greece and Italy. He got bigger, in every sense that matters: bigger books, bigger historical subjects, bigger sales, bigger cultural footprint, a name recognizable to a generation of readers and at least one major anime director who'd never opened the Akutagawa jury notes and wouldn't have known what they were.

**What the Expansion Cost Him**

What that expansion cost him is a harder question, and this volume hasn't tried to answer it with false tidiness. It cost him, for one thing, his easiest critical defense. A writer working entirely inside his own head, as the early trilogy mostly did, can at least be argued over on purely literary terms, exactly the terms the *junbungaku* establishment used against him in 1979 and 1980. A writer whose novel becomes a national event, whose earlier detachment gets relabeled commitment, whose stylistic fingerprint shows up in other writers' prose and other directors' films, is a writer other people now have a stake in, and stakes invite disagreement that has nothing to do with sentence-level craft. Katō's specific objection to the "detachment" label, and Kawamoto's suspicion of the turn toward *shakai-ha* engagement traced back in Chapter 3, are both versions of the same complaint: the bigger Murakami's cultural footprint got, the more his own critics felt entitled to argue not just about what he'd written, but about what he owed the country reading him.

It also cost him, more literally, the option of staying a private, contained writer at all. Volume One ended with him choosing distance on purpose, from a level of attention that had started to feel like exposure rather than reward. This volume ends with that choice no longer really available to him. A writer whose new release gets a printing decision made on launch day, whose earlier alienation has become other people's default vocabulary for their own, doesn't get to simply leave the country and wait for the noise to die down anymore. The noise is the point now, whether he wants it to be or not.

Whether that trade was worth it, from the inside, isn't a question this volume can answer for him. What it can say is what happened: a writer once dismissed as too foreign to belong to his own country's literary tradition ended this stretch of his career as one of the very few things large enough to feel, briefly, like the whole country's tradition at once. The final volume of this series picks up from here, following him into the last fifteen years of his career and into the question of whether a writer this large can still surprise anyone, including himself.

## GLOSSARY

- **Shakai-ha (社会派)** — "Socially engaged": the term critic Saburō Kawamoto used, with real suspicion, to describe Murakami's turn toward direct engagement with real events after *Underground*. See Chapter 3.
- **Detachment (デタッチメント) / Commitment (コミットメント)** — Murakami's own paired terms, discussed in a published dialogue with psychologist Hayao Kawai, for the ethical stance running through his early fiction (a deliberate distance from ideology, rooted in his disillusionment with the failed 1960s student movement) and the shift toward direct social engagement he located around 1995. See Chapters 3 and 7.
- **Murakami Haruki Children (村上春樹チルドレン)** — A loose critical label for younger Japanese writers, including Takayuki Honda and Yoshio Ōsaki, whose prose shows visible traces of Murakami's style, metaphor habits, and recurring motifs. See Chapter 6.
- **The Sheep tetralogy** — An alternate grouping, used by some readers and critics, of *Hear the Wind Sing*, *Pinball, 1973*, *A Wild Sheep Chase*, and *Dance Dance Dance* as a four-book unit, as distinct from the narrower "Trilogy of the Rat" covering only the first three. See Chapter 1 and Volume One, Chapter 5.

## A NOTE ON WHAT COMES NEXT

This is the second of three volumes on Murakami's work. It ends in 2010, with *1Q84* still reverberating and a writer whose private vocabulary of alienation had become, somewhat against his own control, a generation's shared shorthand. The third and final volume covers his more recent novels, a real controversy over a passage touching on the Nanjing Massacre, his decades-long status as literature's most reliable Nobel Prize non-winner, and the globalized, simultaneously released, heavily academically studied phenomenon "Murakami" has become in the years since this volume ends.

## About the Author

Tyler Atsumori was born and raised in Kyoto to an American mother and a Japanese father, and studied modern and contemporary Japanese literature at a university in the Kansai region before continuing an ongoing research focus on Murakami's fiction specifically. This volume, like the first, draws directly on Japanese-language sources most English-language criticism doesn't reach: literary criticism, historical scholarship on the events Murakami's fiction engages with, and Murakami's own essays, read the way they were actually written, alongside the English-language reception more familiar to this book's readers.
